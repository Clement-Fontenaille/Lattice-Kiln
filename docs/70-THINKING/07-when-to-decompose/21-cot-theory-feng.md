# Towards Revealing the Mystery behind Chain of Thought: A Theoretical Perspective

## Density

**Density: HIGH** — Nearly every page is load-bearing: four theorems with full constructive proofs and two circuit-complexity reductions, and the core claim (a computation that a fixed-depth model cannot do in one shot becomes doable when it is split into a chain of small steps, because the chain buys serial depth) is a direct formal statement about why decomposition helps at all, even though the paper never uses the word "decomposition" for agent systems and never studies multi-call orchestration.

## Name

Guhao Feng, Bohang Zhang, Yuntian Gu, Haotian Ye, Di He, Liwei Wang. "Towards Revealing the Mystery behind Chain of Thought: A Theoretical Perspective." NeurIPS 2023 (Oral). arXiv:2305.15408v3 (28 June 2023), 42 pages including appendix.

**Keywords (mine):** expressivity, circuit complexity, TC0 vs NC1, log-precision transformer, effective depth, serial vs parallel computation, autoregressive construction, dynamic programming, topological ordering of sub-problems, length extrapolation.

## Approach

The paper asks an expressivity question, not a learning or prompting question. Footnote 1 states the scope plainly: "CoT" here means the general framework of step-by-step generation, not any particular prompting technique; the paper studies *why* a model that generates intermediate steps can succeed where one that answers directly cannot, and explicitly does not study which prompt triggers that process.

The formal setting is as follows.

**Model class.** A standard decoder-only autoregressive transformer as in Vaswani et al.: an embedding plus positional embedding, then `L` blocks of multi-head softmax self-attention with a causal mask followed by a two-layer pointwise FFN with GeLU activation, both with residual connections. Size is characterised by three quantities: depth `L`, hidden width `d`, number of heads `H`. Generation is the ordinary loop — the last position's final-layer vector predicts the next token, that token is appended to the input, and the process repeats until an end-of-sentence token.

**Precision.** The key realism assumption is the *log-precision* transformer (Section 2, formalised in Appendix A.3): every internal neuron stores a floating-point number with `O(log n)` bits, where `n` bounds the input length. The paper argues this resembles practice (16 or 32 bits against a context of 2048) and avoids the infinite-precision assumption that made earlier Turing-completeness results vacuous. Appendix A.3 also notes the consequence that matters conceptually: a single neuron holds only `O(log n)` bits and therefore *cannot store the full input sequence*, so the computation must be distributed across token positions.

**"Constant size."** In Theorems 3.3 / 3.4 the hidden dimension `d` is independent of the input length `n` (Appendix D.1 / E.1 sharpen it to `d = O(poly(p))`, i.e. it depends on the size of the number field but not on the problem size), depth is a literal constant (5 for arithmetic, 4 for equations), and heads are 5 per layer. What is *not* constant is the numeric magnitude of the parameters: they are bounded by `O(poly(n))`. Remark 3.5 plus Appendix A.3 argue this polynomial bound is exactly what makes the construction implementable in log precision without loss of accuracy.

**"Answer" versus "CoT derivation."** Formally these are two output formats for the same task. Direct answering means the tokens immediately following the input encode the answer ("3", or "x=1;y=1;z=0"). A CoT derivation means the model emits a specified intermediate sequence in a fixed, human-readable math-language format, defined precisely in Appendix B, and the answer is the tail of that sequence. The CoT format is not free-form: it is a formally specified rewriting discipline, and the theorems are about generating *that* string.

**Number field.** To avoid infinitely many floating-point tokens, both math tasks are posed over the finite field of integers modulo a prime `p`, so every number is one of `p` tokens in a fixed dictionary and division is well defined (Appendix A.1). The authors assert this loses no generality.

**What is proved.** Two impossibility results for direct answering on the math tasks (Theorems 3.1, 3.2), two constructive possibility results for CoT on the same tasks (Theorems 3.3, 3.4), one constructive possibility result for a general class of dynamic-programming problems (Theorem 4.7), and one impossibility result for direct answering on a DP problem (Theorem 4.8).

## Model(s) / problem classes targeted, and what the experiments use

**Problem classes in the theory.**

1. `Arithmetic(n, p)` — evaluate an arithmetic expression over numbers, `+ - × ÷` and brackets, of length at most `n`, in the field mod `p`.
2. `Equation(m, p)` — solve a system of `m` linear equations in `m` variables over the same field, assuming the solution is unique.
3. A general class of **dynamic programming** problems, defined by a state space `I` of sub-problems, a transition function `T`, and an aggregation function `A`. The class is restricted (equation 5) so that each state depends on a *constant* number `J` of input tokens and a *constant* number `K` of earlier states: `dp(i) = f(i, s_{g_1(i)},…,s_{g_J(i)}, dp(h_1(i)),…,dp(h_K(i)))`. Aggregation has the restricted form `u(□_{i∈A} dp(i))` where `□` is min, max, or sum.
4. **Context-Free Grammar Membership Testing** (via the CYK algorithm) as the negative DP example.

**Experiments.** Four tasks: Arithmetic, Equation, Longest Increasing Subsequence (LIS), Edit Distance (ED). Models are trained from scratch — not prompted LLMs. Standard transformers via minGPT, hidden dimension 256, 4 heads, FFN hidden size 1024, depths `L ∈ {3,4,5}`, sinusoidal positional embeddings (relative positional encodings for the extrapolation run), AdamW, lr 1e-4, batch 512, dropout 0.1, 100 epochs on 4 V100s. `p = 11` for the math tasks; 150 distinct input tokens for LIS, 26 for ED.

## Author incentive

All six authors are at Peking University (Feng, Zhang, Gu and Ye contributed equally; Di He and Liwei Wang are the senior authors). No funding statement, no industry affiliation, and no product appears in the retrieved version. The position stake is academic: this is a claim to be the first theoretical account of CoT expressivity, and it stakes out territory against the earlier universality and Turing-completeness literature, which it criticises for relying on infinite precision. The paper is deliberately framed as "a first step", which is both a modesty signal and a claim of priority. There is no proprietary model, dataset, or method being promoted — the experiments use an open minGPT and synthetic data.

## Measurement methodology

**On the theory side**, the status of each element is unusually easy to separate, which is a credit to the paper:

- **Theorems 3.1 and 3.2** (impossibility for arithmetic and equations) are **conditional**. Both are stated "Assume TC0 ≠ NC1". They are not unconditional lower bounds. The structure of both proofs is the same: (a) cite Merrill & Sabharwal [36] for the result that bounded-depth log-precision transformers of polynomial size compute only functions in TC0 — this is *imported*, not proved here; (b) prove by reduction that the task is NC1-hard. For arithmetic, the reduction is from Boolean Formula Evaluation, which is NC1-complete by Buss [11]; the translation maps `¬φ → (1−φ)`, `∧ → ×`, `∨ → 1−(1−·)×(1−·)`, and blows up length by only `O(|s|)`. For equations, the reduction is from Unsolvable Automaton Membership Testing, NC1-complete by Barrington [3,4]; the automaton run is encoded as `(n+1)|Q|+1` linear equations whose coefficients each depend on at most one input symbol, so the encoding is itself in NC0. The equation proof adds a step that matters: since the output is multi-token, the authors note one can order the variables so that the model must emit `x*` *first*, and the parallel complexity of producing that first token is bounded by TC0. Both reductions are short and fully written out.
- **Theorem 4.8** (impossibility for CFG membership) is also **conditional**, but on a *weaker and more widely believed* assumption: `TC0 ≠ P`. Its proof is three lines: CFG membership is P-complete (cited, [28]), and log-precision bounded-depth transformers are in TC0, so the problem is out of reach. This is the least worked-out proof in the paper — it is essentially a citation plus one inference.
- **Theorems 3.3 and 3.4** (positive constructions for arithmetic and equations) are **unconditional and constructive**. There is no complexity assumption. The proofs (Appendices D.1, E.1) explicitly build every layer: what each attention head's `Q`, `K`, `V` matrices are, what quantity each MLP computes, and which technical lemma licenses it. They rest on a set of **lemmas proved in Appendix C**: a two-layer GeLU MLP with hidden width 4 approximates scalar multiplication with weights bounded by `poly(M, 1/ε)` (Lemma C.1); MLPs implement conditional selection (C.4) and look-up tables (C.5); attention implements COPY (C.7) and MEAN (C.8) operations. The construction is *approximate*, not exact — every step carries an error `ε`, and the proof closes with an explicit error-propagation argument: because the final output only needs `O(1)` accuracy to decode the right token, choosing a fixed small `ε = Θ(1)` keeps all parameters within `O(poly(n))`.
- **Theorem 4.7** (DP) is constructive but rests on **four explicit assumptions**, which is the weakest-supported of the positive results:
  - **Assumption 4.2**: the state space is polynomially bounded in input length, `|I| = O(poly(|s|))`. Needed so states fit in log precision.
  - **Assumption 4.3**: each of `f`, `g`, `h`, `u` is approximable with *polynomial efficiency* (Definition 4.1 — a robustness-to-input-perturbation condition plus a `O(poly(1/ε))` parameter bound) by a **constant-size** perceptron.
  - **Assumption 4.4**: the *successor function on the topological ordering* — given the problem size vector and state `i_k`, return `i_{k+1}` — is likewise approximable by a constant-size perceptron.
  - **Assumption 4.5**: membership in the aggregation set `A` is likewise decidable by a constant-size perceptron.
  - Remark 4.6 **asserts** these are mild, and Appendix F.1 discharges them concretely for LIS, ED, and CFG membership by writing out the successor function for each. So the assumptions are verified on three instances, not proved for the class.
  - Theorem 4.7 also changes the output convention: it drops tokenisation and works in a **regression setting** where each `(i, dp(i))` pair is a single real vector produced directly by the last layer and projected to the nearest element of a discrete space, then fed straight back as input with no embedding layer. This is a real gap between Theorem 4.7 and Theorems 3.3/3.4, and it is stated openly.

**On the experimental side.** Dependent variable is exact test accuracy (greedy decoding; for CoT runs, the model generates the whole chain and the final step is scored as the answer). Controls: identical architecture, hyperparameters, and dataset provenance across arms — the "direct" datasets are constructed by *deleting the intermediate derivations from the CoT datasets*, so the input/answer distribution is held fixed and only the presence of intermediate steps varies. Depth is varied (`L = 3,4,5`) so that the direct arm is given the advantage of extra depth. N: 1M training and 0.1M test samples per dataset, with train/test duplicates removed; three difficulty levels per task (Arithmetic 4/5/6 operators; Equation 3/4/5 variables; LIS length 50/80/100; ED average string length 12/16/20). Each experiment is repeated **five times with error bars**. No significance test is reported, and no p-values; the claimed gap is large enough that the error bars are the whole argument. Note that the actual accuracy numbers live only in Figures 2 and 3, which are images — the main text quantifies the direct arm as "accuracy mostly below 60%" and the CoT arm as "near-perfect"/"almost perfect" at all difficulty levels, with 3-layer CoT models succeeding where 5-layer direct models fail.

## Key findings

1. **Direct answering is provably out of reach for bounded-depth models on these tasks.** For arithmetic and for linear equations, for any fixed depth `L` and any polynomial `Q`, there is a problem size at which no log-precision transformer of depth `L` and width `d ≤ Q(n)` solves the task — assuming TC0 ≠ NC1. The same holds for CFG membership assuming TC0 ≠ P. Note the exact shape of the statement: it is not "large models fail", it is "for each fixed depth, the *width* would have to grow super-polynomially". Depth is the resource being starved.

2. **The reason is parallel complexity, not total work.** This is the paper's own emphasis, stated in the introduction and again after Theorem 3.1: the obstruction "is not due to the (serialized) computational cost of these problems but rather to their parallel complexity". A fixed-depth transformer is a *well-parallelised* machine — constant depth, polynomial width, i.e. TC0 — and the hard tasks are ones with no efficient parallel algorithm (NC1-hard, or P-complete in the CFG case). Every problem in the paper is trivially easy in serial time; what defeats the model is being forced to do it in constant parallel depth.

3. **CoT converts steps into depth.** The positive results say a *constant-size* model suffices when it emits a derivation: depth 5, 5 heads, width independent of `n` for arithmetic; depth 4, 5 heads for equations; constant `L`, `d`, `H` for DP. The mechanism is stated explicitly at the end of Section 3.2: with CoT the effective depth is no longer `L`, because generated outputs are looped back into the input, and "the dependency between output tokens leads to a significantly deeper circuit with depth proportional to the length of the CoT solution". A fixed circuit re-entered repeatedly escapes TC0. This is the paper's central mechanism and it is a claim about *serial depth bought by autoregression*.

4. **Number of serial steps, as a function of input size.** The paper gives this through the CoT formats in Appendix B rather than as a headline count:
   - **Arithmetic**: a step computes exactly one "handle" — a neighbouring pair of numbers sharing a parent in the syntax tree, with the leftmost handle chosen when several exist — and rewrites the expression with that sub-expression replaced (dropping now-redundant brackets). Each step removes exactly one operator, so the number of derivation steps is the number of operators, i.e. `O(n)` in the input length. But each step re-emits the whole remaining expression, so the *total generated token count* is `O(n²)`.
   - **Equation**: Gaussian elimination, exactly `m−1` steps, each eliminating one variable across all equations. Each step re-writes the whole system, so the total token count is on the order of `m³`.
   - **DP**: exactly one CoT step per state, so `|I|` steps plus a final answer, and `|I|` is polynomial in input length by Assumption 4.2 (quadratic for LIS and ED as formulated; `O(n³·|V|)` for CYK).
   The step count is therefore always a **function of the input**, determined by the algorithm, never a budget, a hyperparameter, or something chosen at runtime. There is no notion in this paper of spending more or fewer steps, of a step budget, or of a cost/accuracy tradeoff.

5. **How bounded each step is.** Very. In the arithmetic construction, whether a given output position needs a computation or merely a copy depends on a **fixed window of five previous tokens** (`e_{j_1}` … `e_{j_5}`), retrieved by five parallel COPY heads keyed on two counters: the number of equal signs so far and the distance to the last equal sign. Layer 1 computes those counters, layers 2–3 fetch the five-token window and decide, layer 4 keeps only the leftmost handle and computes how much the sequence shrank, layer 5 emits either a copied token or the computed outcome. The arithmetic rules themselves are a finite **look-up table** implemented as a two-layer MLP of hidden width `O(p⁶)`. So a step is: locate yourself relative to the previous line, read a constant-size window, do one table lookup or one copy. The DP construction is the same shape: block 1 recovers the sizes of the input segments and computes the *next* state via the successor function; block 2 evaluates `g` and `h` on that next state and sets flags; block 3 uses `J + K` heads to fetch the constant number of needed input tokens and the constant number of needed earlier `dp` values, then applies `f`; block 4 aggregates. Each step touches a constant number of prior results.

6. **Sub-problems map onto CoT steps one-to-one, in topological order.** The generated sequence has the literal form `input_1 | … | input_N | (i_1, dp(i_1)) … (i_{|I|}, dp(i_{|I|})) | answer`, where `(i_1,…,i_{|I|})` is a feasible topological ordering of the DAG induced by the partial order `j ≺ i`. The step format therefore carries **both the sub-problem index and its value**, and this is exactly what makes recombination work: the attention head that retrieves `dp(h_t(i))` finds it by matching on the emitted *state* embedding `e_j^state`. If steps emitted values without their indices, the addressing would fail. Assumption 4.4 is the other half of the requirement — the model must be able to compute *which sub-problem comes next* from the current one and the problem size, in constant size, which is what lets a constant-depth model walk the whole DAG without ever holding it.

7. **Learnability, not just expressivity.** Direct-answer training fails on all four tasks at all difficulty levels even at greater depth, while CoT training reaches near-perfect accuracy from three layers. Increasing depth does help the direct arm somewhat — which the authors note is consistent with their theory, since depth is precisely the scarce resource — but not enough as inputs lengthen.

8. **Length extrapolation.** A three-layer model trained with CoT on arithmetic with 1–15 operators still performs well at 16–18 operators. The authors read this as evidence the model learned the procedure rather than memorising the input/output distribution. This is the one place where the paper touches generalisation, and it is stated as suggestive, not proved.

## Serial depth versus parallel width, and working memory

**Serial depth versus parallel width: yes, and it is the whole point.** The paper's framing is explicitly a depth/width trade. TC0 is defined in Appendix A.2 as constant-depth, unbounded-fan-in, polynomial-size circuits with majority gates; NC1 as `O(log n)`-depth, bounded-fan-in, polynomial-size circuits. The negative results say that on these tasks, width cannot substitute for depth: for any fixed depth, no polynomial width suffices. The positive results say serial generation supplies the missing depth for free — a constant-width, constant-depth model, re-entered `O(n)` or `poly(n)` times, exceeds what any polynomial-width constant-depth model can do in one pass. Appendix A.2 also observes that NC1 still admits decent parallelisability while P-complete problems (CFG membership) do not, which is why the two impossibility results rest on different assumptions.

**Working memory / context length: essentially nothing is proved, and what is said cuts the other way.** The paper proves bounds on *step count*, *depth*, *width*, *heads*, and *parameter magnitude*. It proves no bound on the context a step needs. The relevant statements are indirect:

- Appendix A.3 notes that log precision means each neuron holds only `O(log n)` bits and therefore cannot hold the whole input, so computation "must be somehow distributed on each token". That is a per-neuron statement, not a per-step context bound.
- The constructions *require the full generated prefix to remain visible*. Every mechanism is an attention head reaching back into the history — the five-token window in arithmetic is located by attending over the entire preceding sequence, and the DP construction retrieves `dp(h_t(i))` by attending to whichever earlier position emitted that state. There is no forgetting, no summarisation, no bounded scratchpad. The model's memory *is* the transcript.
- Consequently the *context length* grows with the total CoT length, which is `O(n²)` for arithmetic and `O(m³)` for equations — superlinear in the input, and considerably larger than the input itself. The paper does not flag this as a cost, discuss it, or bound it.

So: the per-step *computation* is proved to be constant-size; the per-step *working memory* is unbounded-and-growing by construction, and unexamined.

## Open questions the paper itself raises

Section 7 lists four:

1. **How CoT generation is triggered by prompts.** The paper studies why step-by-step generation is more expressive, not what makes a model do it. It calls the prompt/output relation valuable and unaddressed.
2. **The role of model size in CoT ability.** It has been observed empirically that scaling improves CoT; the paper's theory says constant size suffices, so it explicitly flags the mismatch as an open theoretical problem.
3. **Generalisation.** The results are about expressivity only. How a model generalises from CoT demonstrations, including out of distribution (the length extrapolation of Figure 3), is left open.
4. **Learning from few or no CoT demonstrations** — whether models can acquire CoT solutions with limited step-by-step training data, or purely from direct data.

## Appreciation

**Mechanism.** The mechanism is precisely identified and, in my reading, the most durable part of the paper. It is: *autoregressive generation converts sequence length into circuit depth*. A fixed-depth network is a fixed-depth circuit; feeding its output back to its input composes it with itself; `k` steps yield roughly `k` times the depth. Therefore any task whose difficulty is *serial* — whose obstruction is that it cannot be flattened into constant parallel depth — becomes reachable by a small model that is allowed to write intermediate results down. The complement is just as sharp and less often quoted: **splitting buys nothing on tasks that are already in TC0**. The theory predicts benefit exactly where the sub-problems form a genuine dependency chain, and the DP framework makes that condition explicit as a partial order with a topological ordering. The paper also identifies the format requirement for recombination — steps must be self-labelling (index plus value) so that later steps can address earlier ones — which falls out of the construction rather than being asserted.

**Magnitude.** Separately, and much weaker. The magnitudes claimed are: constant depth 4 or 5 with 5 heads and width independent of `n`; `O(n)` to `poly(n)` serial steps; parameters bounded by `poly(n)`; experimentally, near-100% with CoT versus "mostly below 60%" without, on four synthetic tasks with 1M training samples each and five seeds. These magnitudes should be held loosely for several reasons. The constructions are hand-built existence proofs, so the constants say what is *sufficient*, not what a trained model does or needs. Width is "constant in `n`" but `O(poly(p))` and includes a look-up table of hidden size `O(p⁶)` — constant only because `p` is fixed. The tasks are synthetic, over a finite field with `p = 11`, with a fixed derivation format and 1M in-format training examples; the direct arm is trained on answers only, which is a very small supervision signal for a hard function, so part of the empirical gap is plausibly an optimisation/supervision effect rather than the expressivity effect the theory describes — the paper does not separate these. And the impossibility results are asymptotic and non-constructive about the crossover: they say "there exists a problem size `n`" at which depth `L` fails, without saying which `n`. Nothing here licenses a quantitative claim about how much any particular decomposition helps at any particular scale.

## How it could serve a harness-design effort / limitations / major concerns

**What it can support.** It gives a principled *criterion* for when serialising a computation should be expected to help: when the task has genuine serial depth — a chain of dependencies that cannot be collapsed — rather than merely being large. It also supplies the structural requirements that make a chain of sub-results composable: a partial order over sub-problems with a fixed topological traversal; a constant fan-in per step (each step reads a bounded number of earlier results); self-labelling step outputs so later steps can address earlier ones; and a successor rule cheap enough to compute from the current position alone. Those four properties are stated as assumptions in the paper, and they read as a checklist for a well-formed decomposition.

**Limitations that matter for that use.**

1. **It is about tokens within one generation, not about calls, agents, or a harness.** Every result concerns a single autoregressive transformer emitting a single sequence. There is no orchestration, no separate sub-agent, no context handoff, no coordination. The "steps" are token positions in one continuous stream, sharing one KV cache. Any transfer to a multi-call system requires assuming a separate call is at least as good as an appended token — which is a real assumption, since the construction depends on unrestricted attention back over the entire prefix, exactly what a fresh call destroys.
2. **No cost model whatsoever.** The Subject asks about the cost of splitting; this paper does not price it. Steps are counted only as an existence quantity. The `O(n²)` and `O(m³)` token growth of the constructions is never discussed as a cost.
3. **The decomposition is given, never discovered.** The CoT format for each task is fixed in advance by the authors (Appendix B), and for DP the state space, transition, aggregation, and topological ordering are inputs to the theorem. Nothing here bears on *deciding whether to split*, on *how deep to go*, or on planning a decomposition at runtime.
4. **The impossibility results are conditional and asymptotic.** They rest on TC0 ≠ NC1 (arithmetic, equations) and TC0 ≠ P (CFG) — both widely believed but unproven — and on Merrill & Sabharwal's TC0 characterisation of log-precision transformers, imported wholesale. They say nothing about finite `n`, so they cannot be used to claim that a given model fails at a given task size.
5. **The DP theorem changes the output convention.** Theorem 4.7 works in a regression setting with vectors fed back directly, not tokens through an embedding and softmax. It is therefore not quite the same object as Theorems 3.3/3.4, and the gap is not closed.
6. **Nothing about the per-step context requirement.** As noted above, the constructions assume the whole transcript stays visible. A harness that summarises, truncates, or hands off between calls is outside what is proved.
7. **The empirical arm cannot separate expressivity from supervision.** Direct and CoT arms differ both in what the model must express and in how much per-example supervision it receives. The theory says the first should be decisive; the experiment cannot show that it was.

## Five citations worth chasing next

1. **Merrill & Sabharwal [36]** — the result that log-precision transformers of bounded depth and polynomial size lie in TC0. This is the load-bearing import for all three impossibility results; the whole negative half of the paper rests on it.
2. **Buss [11], "The Boolean formula value problem is in ALOGTIME"** — the NC1-completeness used in the arithmetic reduction (Theorem 3.1).
3. **Barrington [3] and Barrington & Therien [4]** — the bounded-width branching program / unsolvable automaton results underpinning the linear-equations reduction (Theorem 3.2).
4. **Wei et al. [55]** — finite-precision encoder-decoder transformers approximately simulating Turing machines with bounded computation time; the nearest neighbour to this paper's positive constructions under a realistic precision assumption.
5. **Liu et al. [31], learning automata** — the contrasting result that a shallow non-recursive transformer provably suffices in a restricted setting, useful for mapping exactly where the constant-depth ceiling does and does not bind.

*(Runner-up: Jones & Laaser [28], "Complete problems for deterministic polynomial time" (STOC 1974), the source for the P-completeness of CFG membership and the sole support for Theorem 4.8.)*
