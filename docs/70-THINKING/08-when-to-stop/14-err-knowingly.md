# Answering the Unanswerable Is to Err Knowingly: Analyzing and Mitigating Abstention Failures in Large Reasoning Models

## Density

**Density: MEDIUM** — The paper carries one genuinely load-bearing empirical result — that a linear probe on mid-reasoning activations separates answerable from unanswerable questions well above chance while the model's own final output does not abstain — plus a modest prompt-based fix built on top of it; the mitigation half is thinner than the analysis half and leans on a single family of ill-posed math problems, but the analysis half bears directly on the Subject because it is a controlled study of a model failing to act on a judgement it demonstrably holds internally.

# Approach

The paper is organised as a two-part study: an analysis of *how* large reasoning models (LRMs) fail to abstain, followed by a training-free mitigation.

The analysis part does three things. First, the authors run five open-weight LRMs on unanswerable math problems under a prompt that explicitly licenses abstention ("respond with 'I don't know.' plus an explanation if the question cannot be answered"), and hand-define a three-way taxonomy of what comes back: *correct abstention*, *hallucinated answer* (the model invents the missing condition and solves the problem it wishes it had been given), and *cognitive fixation* (the model never terminates within a 10,000-token budget, looping over reformulations and invalid solution paths even after it has noticed the flaw). Second, they test whether the model nonetheless *knows* the question is broken, at two levels. At the behavioural level they insert stopping points into the reasoning trajectory — the token "wait" for fixation cases, a paragraph break `\n\n` for hallucination cases — and at each point interrupt the model to ask it either for its answer now or for an explanation of why the question is unanswerable. At the latent level they train per-layer linear probes on hidden activations sampled from the reasoning trajectory, to classify answerable versus unanswerable. Third, they measure the model's *confidence* in emitting "I don't know" at those stopping points, and how often it spontaneously emits it, broken down by which of the three response types the trajectory eventually became.

The mitigation part turns the probe into an online monitor. During generation the reasoning stream is segmented into semantically coherent units (clauses, or transitions marked by discourse cues such as "wait"); at each segment boundary the trained linear probe reads the hidden state and estimates the probability that the question is unanswerable. When that probability crosses a threshold, the method fires an *inference-time intervention*: it appends an instructional guidance prompt into the context that restates the possibility that the question may be unanswerable and licenses abstention as a preferred option, combined with an early-exit strategy that stops the model from continuing to reason. Nothing is trained beyond the linear probes themselves; the base model weights are untouched.

# Models targeted and benchmarks / datasets used

**Models.** Five open-weight reasoning models, all mid-sized and all self-hosted (the method needs hidden-state access, so no API model could have been used): R1-Distill-Llama-8B, R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, R1-Distill-Qwen-14B (DeepSeek-R1 distillations), Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-14B. No frontier closed model (o1, R1 itself at full scale) is evaluated, though GPT-o1 and DeepSeek-R1 are named in the introduction as the motivating class. An additional model, Qwen3-32B, appears only as the automatic judge for two of the metrics.

**Datasets.** Two, both consisting of ill-posed math problems:

- **SUM** (Synthetic Unanswerable Math, from Song et al. 2025). Problems are drawn from AIME 1984–2023, AMC pre-2023, Omni-MATH, and Still, then deliberately broken by one of five criteria: key information deletion, ambiguous key information, unrealistic conditions, unrelated objects, and question deletion. The analysis sections use 1,000 randomly sampled problems. The experiments use SUM's manually verified test set: 284 unanswerable and 284 answerable questions.
- **UMWP** (Sun et al. 2024), built on SVAMP, MultiArith, GSM8K, and ASDiv; 5,200 instances in full, from which the authors sample 600 (300 unanswerable, 300 answerable) as their test set.

For unanswerable items in both datasets, GPT-4o generates a short explanation of *why* the item is unanswerable, and that explanation serves as the ground-truth rationale against which Reason Accuracy is scored.

The choice of ill-posed math is deliberate and the authors say so: math problems are hard for LRMs and, unlike open-domain questions, they give an objective criterion for what "unanswerable" means. The whole paper therefore studies one narrow, clean species of unanswerability — a missing or corrupted premise in a formally specified problem — and not, for instance, questions that are unanswerable because they need information the model does not have, or because they are contested, or because the tool the agent needs is unavailable.

# Measurement methodology (brushed)

**Dependent variables.** Four, reported per model per dataset:

1. *Abstention Rate* — the share of unanswerable questions on which the model correctly abstains. This is decided by a string-and-format rule, not by a judge model: a response counts as correct abstention only if it terminates the thinking phase (emits `</think>`) **and** either contains "I don't know" or supplies a `Reason {}` field explaining unanswerability.
2. *Reason Accuracy* — the share of unanswerable questions where the stated rationale is right. Judged automatically by Qwen3-32B against the GPT-4o-generated ground-truth rationale.
3. *Answer Accuracy* — the share of *answerable* questions solved correctly. Also judged by Qwen3-32B. This is the guard variable: it is what tells you whether the abstention gain was bought by making the model timid.
4. *Token Usage* — tokens generated during reasoning, reported separately for answerable and unanswerable items.

The response taxonomy is likewise operationalised by format rules rather than by human or model judgement: *hallucinated answer* means `</think>` was emitted and a `\boxed{}` answer other than "I don't know" was given with no unanswerability reason; *cognitive fixation* means that under the 10,000-token budget the model emitted neither `</think>` nor a `\boxed{}` answer nor a reason. So "cognitive fixation" is defined as budget exhaustion without a terminal commitment — it is a non-termination category, and the paper's claim that the model in that state has often already noticed the flaw rests on the separate stopping-point experiments, not on the category definition.

**What is controlled.** All methods compared (Vanilla, Dynasor-CoT, DEER, Ours) share the same stopping token "wait" as the universal intervention trigger, and the same elicitation string for intermediate answers (`\n **Final Answer**\n\boxed{`). DEER's confidence threshold is 0.95, the value from its original paper. The token budget is 10,000 in the analysis. Both datasets are balanced by construction between answerable and unanswerable items, so the abstention and answer-accuracy variables are measured on disjoint halves of the same test set — the design does let a reader see the trade-off directly rather than only the abstention side.

**Probe training.** 2,000 answerable/unanswerable pairs from SUM for training, 200 pairs for validation, 1,000 randomly sampled token-level activations per question. The probe input is the multi-head attention output before the residual connection, following Li et al. 2023's inference-time-intervention setup. One probe per layer, trained 75 epochs, batch 16,384, learning rate 3e-5; the best layer is selected on the validation set (layer 22 for R1-Distill-Llama-8B, 17 for R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, 30 for R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, 24 for Qwen3-8B, 26 for Qwen3-14B). At inference, per-token probe probabilities are averaged over all tokens generated so far, so the monitor's estimate is a running mean rather than a spot reading.

**N and significance.** N is small and fixed: 284 unanswerable plus 284 answerable on SUM, 300 plus 300 on UMWP, and 1,000 sampled problems in the analysis sections. Every table reports a single point estimate. **There are no error bars, no confidence intervals, no seeds, no repeated runs, and no significance test anywhere in the paper.** Differences of a few points on n≈284 — for example the answerable-accuracy drops that carry the "maintains reasoning performance" claim — are therefore not statistically separated from noise by anything the paper reports. The abstention-rate gains are large enough (tens of points) that this matters much less for the headline result than for the guard variable.

**Contamination, precisely.** The probe is trained on SUM and only on SUM. On SUM this makes the monitor in-distribution: training pairs and the 284-item test set are both drawn from SUM, and while the paper describes the test set as SUM's own manually verified split (so presumably disjoint from the 2,200 training pairs), it never states that disjointness explicitly, and it never says whether the 1,000 problems used for the earlier behavioural analysis overlap the probe's training pool. UMWP is the honest transfer test — a different dataset, different source problems, no probe retraining — and the probe holds up there (AUROC 0.871–0.970, F1 0.809–0.919, in several cases *higher* than on SUM). The one genuine tuning-on-test concern is the decision threshold: it is set per dataset, t = 0.6 for SUM and t = 0.5 for UMWP, with no held-out selection procedure described for that choice.

# Key findings

**1. The failure is large and does not go away with scale.** On SUM, vanilla abstention rates are 16.90% (R1-Distill-Llama-8B), 21.13% (R1-Distill-Qwen-7B), 36.97% (R1-Distill-Qwen-14B), 47.18% (Qwen3-8B), and 54.22% (Qwen3-14B). Most models fail to abstain on more than half of the items. Scale and model family help — the ordering is monotone in size within a family, and Qwen3 beats the R1 distillations — but the authors' reading is that the trend does not rescue the behaviour: "LRMs often fail to abstain from answering unanswerable problems, despite increased model capacity."

**2. Failure has two distinct shapes, and they are not the same failure.** *Hallucinated answer* is a confident wrong commitment: the model invents the missing premise (their example is a 9.99 dollar handling charge that appears nowhere in the problem) and solves the problem it has just fabricated. *Cognitive fixation* is the opposite — no commitment at all: the model burns the entire token budget reformulating and re-attempting, sometimes after it has already articulated that the question is broken. The two also have different trajectory shapes, which is why the authors use different stopping-point markers for each ("wait" for fixation, whose traces are long; a double newline for hallucination, whose traces are short).

**3. The models can articulate the flaw mid-trajectory even when the final output does not.** Interrupting a *failing* trajectory at a stopping point and asking for an answer yields a correct abstention in more than half of cognitive-fixation cases, and in a notable and size-increasing share of hallucinated-answer cases. Because "I don't know" is cheap to emit by chance, the authors additionally ask, at each stopping point, *why* the question is unanswerable — and both failure types yield high rates of correct explanations. Their summary: the ability to assess answerability "exists but is underused in final decisions."

**4. Answerability is linearly decodable from the reasoning trajectory, and the signal sharpens as reasoning proceeds.** Probe classification accuracy rises steadily with the fraction of the reasoning process consumed, exceeding 0.8 for all five models by the end of the trajectory. Full-trajectory probe quality: AUROC 0.879 / 0.912 / 0.908 / 0.925 / 0.936 on SUM and 0.871 / 0.926 / 0.930 / 0.967 / 0.970 on UMWP, across the five models. This is the paper's central evidentiary move, and it is a probe result, not a trace-reading result — the claim of "internal cognition" is cashed out as: a linear classifier on mid-layer attention outputs separates the two classes.

**5. The gap is characterised as insufficient confidence, not as an absent representation.** Two measurements support this. Abstention *confidence* — the geometric-mean token probability of generating "I don't know" at the stopping point, following the DEER formulation — is lower in both failure types than in correct-abstention trajectories. And the *frequency* of spontaneous "I don't know" across stopping points is likewise lower in both failure types. The authors' own reading: "while the LRM may recognize a question as unanswerable, it often lacks sufficient confidence to act upon it... their decision process remains biased toward answering rather than abstaining." So the recognition is present but sub-threshold; it never becomes strong enough to interrupt the reasoning and convert into a terminal abstention.

**6. The mitigation moves abstention a lot and reasoning accuracy a little.** SUM abstention rate, vanilla to ours: 16.90 to 60.92, 21.13 to 73.94, 36.97 to 74.30, 47.18 to 75.27, 54.22 to 78.17. UMWP: 30.67 to 54.67, 47.67 to 77.33, 49.67 to 60.33, 80.00 to 87.33, 82.33 to 92.67. Reason Accuracy rises in step with abstention (SUM: 14.44 to 53.17, 19.37 to 61.86, 33.45 to 62.68, 41.90 to 64.44, 48.24 to 69.01), which the authors read as the intervention improving intermediate reasoning quality and not merely flipping the final token. Answer Accuracy on the *answerable* half moves only slightly and mostly downward: on SUM 61.97 to 60.92, 69.72 to 67.25, 70.42 to 67.96, 60.92 to 61.62, 66.55 to 65.03; on UMWP 77.67 to 77.33, 90.30 to 90.00, 90.00 to 89.67, 94.33 to 93.67, 94.33 to 92.48. The largest single drop is about 2.5 points. Token usage on unanswerable items falls 30-50% relative to vanilla, and falls slightly on answerable items too — the method is cheaper than doing nothing, because it stops the fixation loops.

**7. Both baselines beat vanilla but trade one failure mode for the other.** Dynasor-CoT (stop when the same intermediate answer repeats three times) and DEER (stop when intermediate-answer confidence passes 0.95) both raise abstention over vanilla, and Dynasor-CoT is a respectable second place. But the response-type breakdown shows why they are not enough: their early exits do cut cognitive fixation, and in doing so they *increase* the proportion of hallucinated answers. The authors' explanation is that an early exit without guidance forces a commitment, and a model forced to commit on an under-specified problem invents the missing piece. Their framing: monitoring must be paired with guided intervention, not just with stopping.

**8. The latent monitor outperforms every behavioural monitor.** Table 3 swaps the probe for three signals read off the model's own visible intermediate outputs — *Direct Behavior* (intervene as soon as an intermediate output is "I don't know"), *Consistency* (require three consecutive such outputs), *Confidence Score* (require an "I don't know" above a confidence threshold). All four monitors improve on vanilla, so monitoring as such is what does most of the work; but the latent probe is best and most consistent. On R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, correct abstention is 73.9 with the latent probe versus 41.6 / 35.7 / 31.2 for the three behavioural variants (vanilla 21.1). Direct Behavior is the strongest behavioural rival but is described as too aggressive, and it costs answerable accuracy (58.1 versus 61.9 vanilla on R1-Distill-Llama-8B). Note the ordering: requiring *more* behavioural evidence before intervening (consistency, confidence) makes things worse, not better — the visible signal is scarce, so demanding corroboration of it mostly means never firing.

**9. Ablation: the guidance prompt carries the abstention gain, the early exit carries the fixation fix.** Removing the early exit but keeping the instructional guidance still leaves a large abstention gain (R1-Distill-Llama-8B 43.3 versus 60.9 full and 16.9 vanilla). Removing the instructional guidance but keeping the early exit collapses the gain (26.1) and, critically, *raises* hallucinated answers above vanilla (33.4 versus 27.8), reproducing the baselines' failure mode inside the authors' own method. Early exit is what removes cognitive fixation; guidance is what stops the freed-up model from fabricating. Neither component alone gives the result.

**10. The intervention verifiably closes the gap it was aimed at.** Measuring immediately before and immediately after the intervention point: abstention confidence rises (for example 79.7 to 87.3 on R1-Distill-Llama-8B/SUM, 90.9 to 98.9 on Qwen3-8B/SUM) and the abstention rate at that point multiplies (30.1% to 78.1%, a factor of 2.6; on R1-Distill-Qwen-7B/SUM, 24.5% to 80.6%, a factor of 3.3). This is the paper's most direct evidence that the mechanism is the one claimed: the same trajectory, at the same point, with a short prompt inserted, converts a sub-threshold recognition into an expressed one.

# Open questions the paper itself raises

The paper has **no limitations section**, and its future-work statement is a single sentence at the end of the conclusion: "Future work aims to explore training-time alignment strategies to improve abstention fidelity." That is the only question the authors explicitly pose — they treat the inference-time fix as a patch and locate the real repair in training. Everything else has to be read out of the framing rather than off an explicit list.

Read that way, the paper leaves the following open, in ways it acknowledges in passing:

- **Whether the misalignment is fixable at inference time at all.** Even the authors' best numbers leave 20-40% of unanswerable items unabstained on SUM. The conclusion's turn toward training-time alignment is a concession that the monitor-and-nudge loop has a ceiling.
- **Why the model's abstention confidence is low in the first place.** The paper measures that it is low (Figure 9) and that the intervention raises it (Table 2), but offers no account of what suppresses it — no attribution to RL post-training, to instruction-following priors, or to anything else. The phrase used is that the "decision process remains biased toward answering," which names the bias without explaining its origin.
- **Which failure mode a given intervention should target.** The authors state as an experimental finding that "different types of abstention failures benefit from different intervention strategies," and the ablation bears this out (early exit fixes fixation; guidance fixes hallucination). They do not propose a monitor that distinguishes the two failure types online and selects the matching intervention; their method applies both components unconditionally.
- **Whether the result extends past ill-posed math.** The dataset choice is defended as giving "objectively defined criteria for unanswerability," which is an admission that the finding is demonstrated only where unanswerability is objective. Nothing in the paper tests open-domain, contested, or knowledge-limited unanswerability.
- **How to set the intervention threshold.** t is 0.6 on SUM and 0.5 on UMWP with no procedure given. The paper does not ask how the threshold would be chosen for a new distribution, which is the question anyone deploying the monitor would face first.

# Appreciation

## MECHANISM - is the causal story the paper tells actually established?

The paper's mechanism claim has three links, and they are not equally well established.

**Link 1: a representation of answerability exists in the reasoning trajectory.** This is well established, by the probe. A linear classifier on one layer's attention output separates answerable from unanswerable at AUROC 0.87-0.97, and it transfers to a dataset it was not trained on. Linear decodability is a strong form of "the information is there", and the transfer to UMWP rules out the probe having memorised SUM's five corruption templates.

But there is an ambiguity the paper does not address, and it is the important one. Answerability here is a **property of the question**, not a state the model arrives at. A missing premise is present in the prompt from token zero. A probe that separates the two classes may therefore be reading a feature of the input that the model encodes as a matter of course, whether or not that feature participates in the model's decision to answer. The paper's own Figure 8 partly speaks to this, because accuracy *rises* with reasoning progress rather than being flat and high from the start, which suggests the signal is at least partly constructed during reasoning rather than merely copied from the prompt. But the paper never reports probe accuracy on the prompt alone, before any reasoning tokens. Without that baseline, "the model internally recognises the flaw" and "the flaw is linearly decodable from a model processing the text" are not separated.

**Link 2: the recognition is available to the model's own output pathway, not merely to an external probe.** This is the link the paper handles best, and it is what raises the work above a pure probing result. The stopping-point experiments interrupt a *failing* trajectory and get a correct abstention out of the model more than half the time in fixation cases, and, crucially, they also get a correct *explanation* of why the question is broken. That second measurement is the right control, and the authors say why they added it: "I don't know" is cheap and could be produced at random, whereas a correct rationale cannot. So the recognition is not probe-only; the model can verbalise it on demand.

**Link 3: what blocks the recognition from surfacing is insufficient confidence, and adding confidence is what the intervention supplies.** This is the weakest link, and it is where the brief's question lands, namely whether this is a representation that exists but is not surfaced, or a reflex that overrides it. The paper's answer is neither cleanly: it says the recognition is present but **sub-threshold**, that abstention signals are "generally not strong enough to interrupt reasoning". The supporting measurement is the geometric-mean token probability of emitting "I don't know" at stopping points, which is lower in both failure types than in correct-abstention trajectories.

That measurement is consistent with the claim but does not isolate it. Abstention confidence being lower in trajectories that end in failure is close to a definitional consequence of those trajectories ending in failure: the model that did not abstain was, by construction, less inclined to abstain. The finding is descriptive of the same fact under a different measurement, not an independent cause of it. Nothing here rules out the alternative that the answering behaviour is a trained prior which outcompetes an equally strong abstention representation. On that reading, "low abstention confidence" is the shadow of the competing pressure rather than the deficit itself.

Two pieces of evidence do push beyond correlation, and they are the paper's best. The first is the **pre/post intervention measurement** in Table 2: the same trajectory, at the same point, with a short prompt inserted, moves from 30.1% to 78.1% abstention and from 79.7 to 87.3 confidence. That is a within-trajectory manipulation, which is much stronger than a between-group comparison. The second is the **ablation's hallucination result**: removing guidance while keeping early exit pushes hallucinated answers *above* vanilla (33.4 against 27.8). A model that had no answerability representation would not behave differently under exit-with-guidance versus exit-without. The fact that guidance is what determines whether the forced commitment becomes an abstention or a fabrication says the representation is there and is being routed, not created, by the prompt.

**Where the mechanism story is strongest:** the demonstration that *stopping is not enough*. The baselines cut fixation and increase hallucination, and the ablation reproduces that failure inside the authors' own method. This is a real mechanistic finding and it is cleanly isolated. An early exit is a forcing function, and forcing a commitment on an underdetermined problem produces a fabricated premise. The two failure modes trade against each other unless something tells the model that "no answer" is an admissible output.

**Where it is weakest:** the interpretive vocabulary outruns the measurements. "Cognitive capabilities", "cognitive monitoring", "cognitive fixation", "internal cognition", "err knowingly" - the cognitive framing is doing rhetorical work that the operational definitions do not support. "Cognitive fixation" in particular is defined purely as budget exhaustion without a terminal token, and the claim that the model in that state has already noticed the flaw comes from a different experiment. The category itself carries no evidence of noticing anything.

## MAGNITUDE - how big are the effects, and how much should be read into them?

**Large and unambiguous: the abstention gains.** 16.9 to 60.9, 21.1 to 73.9, and 37.0 to 74.3 on SUM. The smallest relative gain across five models and two datasets is Qwen3-14B on UMWP (82.3 to 92.7), and even that closes more than half of the remaining gap. These are three- and four-fold improvements on the weaker models. No plausible amount of run-to-run noise on n=284 touches effects of that size. The probe quality is likewise unambiguous, since AUROC in the high 0.8s and 0.9s is not a marginal signal.

**Large and unambiguous: the token savings.** The method uses 30-50% fewer reasoning tokens on unanswerable items. It is not a safety tax; it pays for itself by cutting the fixation loops, which were the most expensive trajectories in the vanilla condition.

**Small and undefended: the guard variable.** The claim that reasoning performance is "maintained" rests on answerable-set accuracy moving by 0 to 2.5 points, almost always downward, on n=284 or n=300, with a single run and no error bars. A 2.5-point difference on n=284 is roughly seven items. The paper reports no seeds, no repeats, no intervals and no significance test, so "maintained" is a claim that its own measurements are not powered to make. It may well be true, since the direction is consistent across ten model-dataset cells, which is itself weak evidence. But it is asserted rather than shown. This is the paper's single most significant methodological weakness, and it sits precisely on the trade-off that a reader most wants quantified.

**Moderate, and the more interesting number: the monitor comparison.** The latent probe beats the best behavioural monitor by 8 points on R1-Distill-Llama-8B (60.9 against 53.1) and by 32 points on R1-Distill-Qwen-7B (73.9 against 41.6). But all four monitors beat vanilla substantially, so the honest reading is that **most of the benefit comes from monitoring at all**, and the probe is an improvement on top of that rather than the source of the effect. The paper's own framing over-credits the latent component; its Table 3 does not.

**Instructive inversion.** Among the behavioural monitors, demanding *more* evidence before firing makes the results *worse* (Direct 41.6, then Consistency 35.7, then Confidence 31.2 on R1-Distill-Qwen-7B). The visible abstention signal is so scarce that any corroboration requirement mostly amounts to never intervening. This is a small table, but it carries a general lesson about thresholding on rare signals.

**Bounded: the ceiling.** Even after intervention, one to four items in ten still go unabstained. The method reduces a failure; it does not close it.

# How it could serve a harness-design effort, limitations, major concerns

## What transfers to a harness

**1. A stall is not a signal that the loop has learned nothing.** The most directly usable finding is that a trajectory which has run out of budget without committing is not an empty trajectory. In the fixation cases, interrupting the model at a "wait" token and asking for its answer produced a correct abstention more than half the time, and a correct explanation of the flaw at a high rate. A harness that treats budget exhaustion as a plain timeout, and discards or retries the attempt, is throwing away a judgement the loop had already formed. The cheap version of the paper's method, which needs no probe at all, is to interrupt a stalling loop and ask it directly whether the task is well-posed, rather than letting it run to the wall.

**2. Stopping a loop and telling it what stopping means are two different jobs, and doing only the first backfires.** This is the finding with the widest bearing on the Subject. Both baselines, and the paper's own ablation without guidance, cut non-termination and *increased* confident fabrication. A stopping criterion is a forcing function, and a loop forced to commit on an underdetermined task will supply the missing premise itself. Any harness that adds a budget cap, a step limit or a repetition detector inherits this: capping the loop converts stalls into confident wrong outputs unless the exit path explicitly licenses "cannot be done" as a valid terminal state. The exit must carry the vocabulary of failure, not merely the instruction to finish.

**3. The two failure shapes need different handling and are separately detectable.** Fabrication is a premature commitment and stalling is an absent one. In this paper they even have different trajectory signatures, long traces with repeated "wait" for fixation and short traces for hallucination, which is why the authors use different stopping-point markers for each. A harness that classifies its own failures into "committed too early on an underdetermined task" versus "never committed" gets to apply different remedies: guidance for the first, a bounded exit for the second. The paper's ablation shows each remedy fixes its own mode and not the other.

**4. Watching the model's visible self-reports is a weak monitor, but it is not a useless one, and the naive version beats the careful one.** For a harness without hidden-state access, which is the ordinary case behind an API, Table 3 is the relevant table. The behavioural monitors do work: Direct Behavior takes R1-Distill-Llama-8B from 16.9 to 53.1 correct abstention, against 60.9 for the latent probe. That is most of the benefit from a signal any harness can read. The counter-intuitive part is that requiring corroboration before acting on it, either three consecutive "I don't know" outputs or one above a confidence threshold, makes things monotonically worse. When the signal you are waiting for is rare, a corroboration rule mostly converts into a rule that never fires. A harness should act on a first self-doubt signal rather than wait for a second.

**5. Guidance is a prompt, not a model change, and it fires mid-trajectory.** The intervention is an appended paragraph reminding the model that the question may be unanswerable and that abstaining is acceptable. It is injected into the context at a segment boundary during generation, not placed in the system prompt at the start. That distinction matters, because the licence to abstain was already in the initial prompt in every condition, including vanilla, and vanilla still failed most of the time. A standing instruction that abstention is permitted does not produce abstention; the same instruction delivered at the moment the loop is wavering does. For a harness, this argues for mid-run injection over up-front policy text.

**6. Abstention machinery does not have to cost tokens.** Token usage fell 30-50% on unanswerable items and slightly on answerable ones. The usual objection to a reliability check, that it adds overhead, does not apply to a mechanism whose main effect is to cut short the loops that were burning the most budget.

## Limitations and major concerns

**The unanswerability studied is not the one an agent loop meets.** This is the largest gap between the paper and the Subject. Every item is an ill-posed math problem whose defect, a deleted premise, an unrealistic condition, an absent question, is fully contained in the prompt and is decidable by inspecting the text alone. The authors chose this deliberately, for the objective criterion. But an agent loop's "beyond me" judgements are almost never of this shape: the task is well-posed and the tool is missing, the credential fails, the environment differs from what was assumed, or the required information sits outside the model. Those are unanswerable-in-context rather than unanswerable-in-text, and the property is not present in the prompt for a probe to find at all. Nothing in this paper shows the mechanism generalises to that case, and the fact that the target property is prompt-resident here is a reason to expect it may not.

**"Done", "failing" and "beyond me" are not separated.** The paper studies exactly one judgement, whether the input is answerable, and it is a fixed property of the item known before generation starts. The Subject's harder question, which is whether the *current attempt* is going anywhere, is not addressed. The probe never estimates progress; it estimates a static label. So the work bears on the abstention half of the Subject and barely on the stopping-criterion half, despite superficially being about when to stop.

**The single-turn setting has no consequences.** The model abstains and the episode ends. There is no downstream cost to a wrong abstention beyond a scored metric, no handoff, no retry with a different approach, and no state that a premature give-up would leave behind. A harness pays real costs for both error directions, and the paper's evaluation cannot see either.

**No statistical treatment at all.** Single runs, single point estimates, no seeds, no intervals, no tests. The abstention gains survive this easily. The "maintains reasoning performance" claim, which is the whole safety argument for deploying the method, does not: it rests on differences of a handful of items.

**Only self-hosted mid-sized models.** Five models between 7B and 14B, all open-weight, because the method needs hidden states. The frontier reasoning models named in the introduction as the motivating case (GPT-o1, full-scale DeepSeek-R1) are never evaluated. Since abstention improves with scale in the results shown, the size of the problem at the frontier is unmeasured, and the latent-monitor half of the method is inapplicable there in any case.

**Judge and ground truth share a pipeline.** GPT-4o writes the ground-truth rationale for why an item is unanswerable, and Qwen3-32B scores the model's rationale against it. Reason Accuracy is therefore a model-judged match against a model-written reference, with no reported human validation of either side.

**A threshold set per dataset with no held-out procedure.** t = 0.6 on SUM, t = 0.5 on UMWP, chosen without a described selection method. This is the one place where a tuning-on-test concern is genuine, and it is the parameter that most directly controls the abstention/accuracy trade-off the paper reports.

**Format-rule metrics.** Abstention Rate is decided by string and format matching, requiring a terminating tag plus either the literal phrase or a Reason field. A model that recognises the flaw and says so in different words scores as a failure. This is a conservative bias for the vanilla condition, but it also means the intervention is partly rewarded for teaching the model the expected output format rather than the judgement.

**Two baselines, neither designed for this.** The authors state plainly that there are no directly comparable methods and that Dynasor-CoT and DEER target answerable questions. The comparison is honest about this, but it means the method's margin is measured against tools being used outside their purpose.

# Five citations worth chasing next

**1. Kirichenko, Ibrahim, Chaudhuri, Bell (2025). "AbstentionBench: Reasoning LLMs Fail on Unanswerable Questions." arXiv:2506.09038.**
This is the benchmark paper behind the phenomenon, and it is the one that most directly repairs this review's largest complaint. Where the paper under review restricts itself to ill-posed math for the sake of an objective criterion, AbstentionBench is built to span multiple kinds of unanswerability, so it is the place to find out whether the "recognises but does not abstain" pattern holds when the defect is not a deleted premise sitting in the prompt. It also reports the frontier and API models that this paper could not evaluate. Read first.

**2. Song, Shi, Zhao (2025). "The Hallucination Tax of Reinforcement Finetuning." arXiv:2505.13988.**
The source of the SUM dataset and of its five corruption criteria, so it is required reading for anyone judging the construct validity of the main results here. More importantly, it offers a causal account that this paper conspicuously lacks: it attributes the answering bias to reinforcement finetuning. The paper under review measures that abstention confidence is low but never explains why, and this citation is where the explanation is attempted. It is also the natural place to check whether the training-time alignment that the conclusion defers to future work has already been examined.

**3. Li, Patel, Viegas, Pfister, Wattenberg (2023). "Inference-Time Intervention: Eliciting Truthful Answers from a Language Model." NeurIPS.**
The methodological parent. The probe input, the pre-residual multi-head attention output, the per-layer probe design, and the training hyperparameters are all taken from here. It matters for a further reason: the original ITI *steers activations* along the probe direction, whereas this paper only *reads* the probe and then intervenes with a text prompt. The difference between reading a direction and pushing along it is the untested design axis in the mitigation, and this citation is where the alternative lives.

**4. Slobodkin, Goldman, Caciularu, Dagan, Ravfogel (2023). "The Curious Case of Hallucinatory (Un)answerability: Finding Truths in the Hidden States of Over-Confident Large Language Models." EMNLP.**
The direct precedent for the paper's central evidentiary move: unanswerability encoded in hidden states while the visible output is confidently wrong. Reading it establishes how much of this paper's probing result is new versus an extension of a known finding into reasoning models, and it is the place to look for the control this paper omits, namely whether the answerability signal is present in the prompt representation before any generation begins. That control is what separates "the model recognises the flaw" from "the flaw is decodable from a model reading the text".

**5. Yang, Si, Duan, Zhu, Zhu, Lin, Cao, Wang (2025). "Dynamic Early Exit in Reasoning Models" (DEER). arXiv:2504.15895.**
The most load-bearing of the two baselines, and the source of both the confidence formulation used throughout the analysis and the early-exit half of the mitigation. It is the closest thing in the paper's bibliography to a stopping-criterion method for an iterative loop, which makes it the citation with the most direct bearing on the Subject's stopping-criterion half. Worth pairing with Fu et al. (2025), "Reasoning without Self-Doubt: More Efficient Chain-of-Thought through Certainty Probing" (Dynasor-CoT), the consistency-based sibling, which was the stronger of the two baselines in these results.
