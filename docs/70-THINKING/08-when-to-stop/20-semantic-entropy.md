# Semantic Uncertainty / Semantic Entropy (Kuhn, Gal, Farquhar; and the Nature 2024 follow-up)

## Density

**Density: HIGH.** Almost every section of this paper carries load-bearing content: the conceptual argument (meaning is the invariance we care about, tokens are not), the method (three concrete steps, with pseudocode), and an ablation-heavy evaluation that also strengthens its own baselines rather than weakening them. The share of that signal which bears directly on the review Subject is narrower, because the paper studies a single finished answer to a single question and never a loop, but the parts that do bear on the Subject are strong: it gives a cheap, unsupervised, sample-based "do not trust this output" score, it measures that score against model self-report (`p(True)`), and it states plainly where the signal breaks.

## Approach

The paper starts from a mismatch between what a language model gives you and what you want to know. A model gives you token likelihoods, so the entropy you can compute directly is an entropy over token sequences. What you actually care about is whether the model is unsure about the *content* of its answer. Those two quantities come apart because many different sequences mean the same thing. The authors' own example: a model torn between "France's capital is Paris" and "Paris is France's capital" looks uncertain at the token level while being completely certain in every sense that matters. They call this problem semantic equivalence, and they name the distinction the way linguists do — the semantic content of a sentence against its syntactic and lexical form. Lexical equivalence entails the other two; the implication does not run backwards.

Their fix is to move the entropy into meaning-space. Formally, they posit a semantic equivalence relation `E(·,·)` that holds between any two sentences meaning the same thing. Because it is an equivalence relation (reflexive, symmetric, transitive), it partitions the space of sequences into equivalence classes, and each class is one possible meaning. The probability of a meaning is then the sum of the sequence probabilities inside its class:

    p(c|x) = Σ_{s∈c} p(s|x) = Σ_{s∈c} Π_i p(s_i | s_<i, x)

In the paper's own framing, this treats the output variable as living in the event-space `C`, a sub-σ-algebra of the standard sequence event-space `S`.

The method has exactly three steps.

1. **Generation.** Sample `M` sequences from one model's predictive distribution given the context `x`, by multinomial sampling (or, in one ablation, multinomial beam sampling). A single unmodified off-the-shelf model — no ensemble, no Bayesian or stochastic modification, no fine-tuning. They note that ensembling would probably help but that training several independent foundation models is usually prohibitive.
2. **Clustering.** Group the samples by meaning using their bidirectional entailment algorithm (detailed in the next section).
3. **Entropy estimation.** Sum the likelihoods within each cluster to get a per-meaning likelihood, then take the entropy over that meaning-distribution:

       SE(x) = − Σ_c p(c|x) log p(c|x)

   Since the full set of possible meanings is not available, they estimate the expectation by Monte Carlo integration over the clusters actually found, `SE(x) ≈ −|C|^{-1} Σ_i log p(C_i|x)`, which they state is an unbiased estimator of the entropy. In some settings they additionally length-normalise.

Table 1 of the paper is the whole idea in miniature. For "What is the capital of France?", if the samples are Paris (0.5) / Rome (0.4) / London (0.1), clustering changes nothing and both entropies are 0.94. If instead they are Paris (0.5) / "It's Paris" (0.4) / London (0.1), the first two merge into one meaning at 0.9 and the entropy falls from 0.94 to 0.33 — which is the honest reading of a model that is in fact fairly sure.

### The entailment and clustering step

Semantic equivalence is operationalised as **bidirectional entailment**: `s` means the same as `s'` if and only if each logically implies the other. Crucially, entailment is judged *with respect to the context*, because meaning often sits partly in the context. Their example is that bare "Paris." does not entail "The capital of France is Paris." in isolation, since "Paris." is not a declarative sentence on its own — but within the question's context, the one-word answer does entail the fuller one.

Any natural language inference (NLI) classifier can fill this slot. They use a **Deberta-large model fine-tuned on MNLI** (1.5B parameters). For each pair of samples they concatenate context-plus-answer for both, join the two with a special token, and read off the classifier's label among `entailment` / `neutral` / `contradiction`. They run it in both directions and return `equivalent` only when both directions come back `entailment`. Algorithm 1 gives the pseudocode: sequences are processed one at a time and compared against the *first* member of each already-formed class; anything matching no class starts its own.

**Reliability.** Because the component is new, they validate it by hand. They manually label 300 generations per dataset from the 13B model and find the classifier's semantic-equivalence accuracy at **92.7% on TriviaQA and 95.3% on CoQA**. They are candid about a formal wrinkle: their implementation is slightly "soft" and sometimes calls not-quite-equivalent pairs equivalent, so the relation is not strictly transitive, so the set of equivalence classes is not strictly unique and the clusters can in principle depend on comparison order. They report that in practice this is not a noticeable problem and flag it as an area for better clustering algorithms.

**Cost.** The pairwise algorithm is combinatorial in `M`, needing `(M choose 2)` comparisons in the worst case, but the authors argue the cost is small next to generation for three reasons: `M < 20` is usually enough; the Deberta model is far smaller than the generating model, so one pair comparison is cheaper than emitting a single token from the main model; and transitivity means only one member per class needs comparing. Empirically the number of clusters is low (Table 2), which keeps the real comparison count well under the worst case.

## Models targeted and datasets used

- **Models.** The GPT-like open **OPT** family (Zhang et al., 2022) at **2.7B, 6.7B, 13B and 30B** parameters, with 1.3B appearing in the accuracy ablation of Appendix B.4. Headline numbers use the 30B model, described as the largest computationally feasible. The models are used in pre-trained form, not fine-tuned on either dataset. The authors state semantic entropy works with any similar model; OPT is simply what they could run openly. Hardware: 80GB NVIDIA A100s.
- **Datasets.** **CoQA** (Reddy et al., 2019) as *open-book* conversational QA, where a supporting paragraph is available — development split, roughly 8000 questions, zero-shot prompting. **TriviaQA** (Joshi et al., 2017) as *closed-book* QA with no supporting paragraph — an 8000-question subset of the training split chosen to match CoQA's size, 10-shot prompting. Model accuracy is 82.3% on CoQA against 50.6% on TriviaQA, so TriviaQA is the harder setting.
- **Task framing.** Free-form QA is chosen deliberately: it is a difficult and high-stakes use of generation, yet ground truth is cheaper to establish than for something like summarisation.

## Author incentive

The three authors are one academic group — OATML, Computer Science, Oxford — with Yarin Gal as the senior Bayesian-deep-learning figure and Sebastian Farquhar as the author who later leads the Nature paper. No funder, company, or product is named in the paper; the acknowledgements thank Geoffrey Irving, Kuba Perlin, Laura Rimell, and Miles Turpin plus OATML members. The visible stake is intellectual and methodological rather than commercial, and the paper states it openly in two places. The reproducibility statement argues that existing NLG-uncertainty research leans on proprietary models, expensive fine-tuning, and human evaluation, which puts the field out of reach for academic groups; their answer is an entirely open-source pipeline on the newly released OPT models, with no fine-tuning, released under MIT. That is a position stake in *unsupervised, off-the-shelf* uncertainty and against supervised self-evaluation approaches, and the framing of the baselines follows from it. One admission is worth recording as a constraint on their own comparison: they say they could not implement one of Kadavath et al.'s proposals (training a model to predict confidence directly) because of hardware limitations, and their `p(True)` reimplementation is capped at 10-shot prompting on a 30B open model where the original used 20-shot on a proprietary 52B model.

## Measurement methodology (brushed)

**The dependent variable is AUROC**, not calibration error. The task is framed as a binary detection problem: given an uncertainty score for one generation, predict whether that generation is incorrect. AUROC is then, in their words, the probability that a randomly chosen correct answer has a lower uncertainty score than a randomly chosen incorrect one; 1.0 is perfect and 0.5 is chance. They argue explicitly *against* calibration measures such as the Brier score here, and the argument is a consequence of their own thesis: to score calibration you would need the probability mass assigned to the whole meaning "the correct answer", summed over every possible way of phrasing it, and that is intractable in free-form text. Entropy survives where the Brier score does not, because entropy is structured as an expected information and therefore admits Monte Carlo integration.

**Correctness labels come from fuzzy string matching.** An answer counts as correct when `Rouge-L(s, s') > 0.3` against the reference. This label is itself validated against humans: they hand-label 200 answers of the 30B model per dataset and find the automatic label agrees with the human label 89% of the time on CoQA and 96% on TriviaQA (Table 5). They then test whether the threshold matters. On TriviaQA (Table 6), moving to Rouge-L > 0.5, Rouge-1 > 0.5, or exact matching swings measured task accuracy from 50.6% down to 39.4% while semantic entropy's AUROC barely moves (0.828, 0.835, 0.835, 0.828), and the gap over normalised entropy stays roughly constant. On CoQA (Table 7) the pattern holds except under exact matching, which is too strict for a task with much greater answer variability and drags both AUROCs down together (0.675 vs 0.673).

**What is controlled and swept.** Model size is swept across four (sometimes five) sizes on both datasets. Sample count `M` is swept, with the finding that beyond 10 samples the measure does not improve significantly — established on 2.7B/6.7B/13B CoQA runs up to 20 samples, after which 10 samples per question is used throughout. Sampling temperature is swept over {0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5} on both datasets. Sampling method is compared (multinomial against multinomial beam search). Accuracy metric is swept as above. The generation used *for scoring correctness* is obtained separately by beam search (`num_beams = 5`), while the answer set used for uncertainty comes from multinomial sampling, so the answer being judged and the samples estimating its uncertainty are not produced identically. TriviaQA generations are trimmed by pattern-matching stop-words (`Q:`, `Question:`, `QUESTION:`, `questions:`) where the model keeps continuing the few-shot pattern.

**N and significance.** N is roughly 8000 questions per dataset, with 10 generations each, plus small hand-labelled sets (300 pairs per dataset for the entailment classifier, 200 answers per dataset for the accuracy metric, 100 classifications for the high-temperature failure analysis). Significance is treated informally. There are no confidence intervals, error bars, or hypothesis tests anywhere in the results I read; the word "significantly" is used descriptively of gaps in the AUROC curves. The evidence is a consistent direction of effect repeated across four model sizes, two datasets, four accuracy metrics, four temperatures, and two sampling methods, rather than any stated statistical test.

## Key findings

**1. Semantic entropy beats every baseline they test at predicting whether an answer is wrong.** On TriviaQA with the 30B model, the ordering in Figure 1a is semantic entropy on top, then normalised entropy, then plain entropy and lexical similarity, with `p(True)` last by a wide margin — the authors say semantic entropy does "dramatically better" than `p(True)`. Roughly, semantic entropy sits near 0.83 AUROC against about 0.80 for normalised entropy on TriviaQA, and 0.77 against 0.75 on CoQA (Tables 6 and 7).

**2. The advantage grows with model size.** Figures 1b and 2 show semantic entropy improving steadily from 2.7B to 30B and pulling further ahead of the baselines as the model gets larger, while still doing acceptably at small sizes. Appendix B.4 adds the context that task accuracy also rises with size, and that at the smallest size (1.3B) both accuracy and uncertainty quality degrade together.

**3. The mechanism is visible in the cluster counts.** Table 2 reports the average number of semantically distinct answers among 10 samples: 1.27 for correctly answered CoQA questions against 1.77 for incorrect ones, and 1.89 against 3.89 on TriviaQA. This is the paper's own account of *why* the method works — the model spreads its samples across more distinct meanings exactly when it is about to be wrong. Notably, the bare cluster count used alone is already a usable uncertainty score (AUROC 0.66 on CoQA, 0.79 on TriviaQA), though weaker than full semantic entropy (0.77 and 0.83). The gap is larger on CoQA, which they attribute to that task's difficulty producing greater spread in the probabilities within the clusters.

**4. It uses extra samples better than sequence entropy does.** Figure 3a shows the gap between semantic entropy and length-normalised entropy *widening* as the sample count grows from 5 to 10, on both datasets. Their explanation is duplication: additional samples that merely restate an existing meaning inflate sequence entropy but are correctly absorbed into an existing cluster.

**5. Sampling temperature is a real hyperparameter of uncertainty estimation, and 0.5 is the sweet spot.** Raising temperature raises sample diversity (measured as 1 minus average pairwise Rouge-L) but lowers average sample correctness; these two forces compete, and AUROC peaks at an intermediate temperature of 0.5 for both semantic entropy and normalised entropy, on TriviaQA (Figure 3b) and identically on CoQA (Figure 4) despite the large accuracy difference between them — which the authors read as a sign the choice may generalise. This finding cuts against prior work: they point out that using temperature 1.0, as Kadavath et al. do for the predictive-entropy baseline, significantly weakens that baseline relative to their own implementation. So the comparison here is deliberately harder on themselves than the literature's default.

**6. Far fewer samples are needed than prior work presumes.** `M = 10` is sufficient; going to 20 does not significantly help. This is one of the three contributions they claim in the introduction.

**7. Where the method breaks, it breaks through the clustering step.** At temperature 1.5 semantic entropy actually *underperforms* length-normalised entropy on CoQA. They diagnose this by hand-labelling 100 classifications at that temperature: the generations degenerate into nonsensical combinations of words lifted from the context, and while their raw likelihoods remain somewhat predictive, semantic clustering becomes very hard. Entailment-classifier accuracy falls to **61%** at T=1.5 against over 92% at lower temperatures. The uncertainty signal is therefore only as good as the NLI model's grip on the text it is given.

**8. Beam search hurts.** Multinomial beam search sampling gives AUROC 0.735 with answer diversity 0.258, against 0.758 and 0.490 for plain multinomial sampling (Table 4). The authors conjecture beam search concentrates too much on the most likely sequences; its diversity matches the lowest-temperature point of the sweep.

**9. Margin probability fails, and fails for a reason that supports the thesis.** The margin between the top-1 and top-2 beam results (Lin et al., 2022b) reaches only 0.54 AUROC on CoQA with the 13B model. Their diagnosis, in Appendix B.6, is that it discards the magnitude of the likelihood, and that it cannot tell paraphrase-uncertainty from meaning-uncertainty. Table 8 is the cleanest single illustration in the paper: "Thomas Edison." versus "Edison." gives a large margin of 0.90 but semantic entropy 0.10, while "Thomas." versus "George." gives margin 0.36 but semantic entropy 0.87. The margin is loudest exactly where the model is confident about the meaning.

**The authors' own reading.** They present the result as evidence that the crucial invariance in natural language — distinct tokens meaning the same thing — should be addressed head-on, and that doing so yields an unsupervised, reproducible, deployable method needing no training or labels. They position semantic entropy as a *middle ground* between purely probabilistic methods and methods that ask the language model to judge itself, and argue this joint approach is more promising than either alone as models improve. They also make the case that unsupervised measures may sidestep the distribution-shift fragility observed in supervised uncertainty methods.

## The Nature 2024 follow-up: what it adds

Farquhar, Kossen, Kuhn and Gal, "Detecting hallucinations in large language models using semantic entropy," Nature 630, 625-630, published 19 June 2024, open access. The first three authors contributed equally. Competing-interests note: Sebastian Farquhar is employed by Google DeepMind and Lorenz Kuhn by OpenAI, with the paper written under their Oxford affiliation; Gal is supported by a Turing AI Fellowship funded through UK Research and Innovation (EP/V030302/1). Peer review was handled with Mirella Lapata as a named reviewer. I retrieved the full article text including Methods, but not the Supplementary Information, so where the paper defers a detail to a Supplementary Note I record only that it does so.

### Verbatim abstract

> Large language model (LLM) systems, such as ChatGPT or Gemini, can show impressive reasoning and question-answering capabilities but often 'hallucinate' false outputs and unsubstantiated answers. Answering unreliably or without the necessary information prevents adoption in diverse fields, with problems including fabrication of legal precedents or untrue facts in news articles and even posing a risk to human life in medical domains such as radiology. Encouraging truthfulness through supervision or reinforcement has been only partially successful. Researchers need a general method for detecting hallucinations in LLMs that works even with new and unseen questions to which humans might not know the answer. Here we develop new methods grounded in statistics, proposing entropy-based uncertainty estimators for LLMs to detect a subset of hallucinations—confabulations—which are arbitrary and incorrect generations. Our method addresses the fact that one idea can be expressed in many ways by computing uncertainty at the level of meaning rather than specific sequences of words. Our method works across datasets and tasks without a priori knowledge of the task, requires no task-specific data and robustly generalizes to new tasks not seen before. By detecting when a prompt is likely to produce a confabulation, our method helps users understand when they must take extra care with LLMs and opens up new possibilities for using LLMs that are otherwise prevented by their unreliability.

*(Reference-superscript numerals present in the published abstract have been removed; the wording is otherwise unaltered.)*

### The 'confabulation' framing

This is the most consequential conceptual addition, and it is a narrowing rather than a broadening. The paper carves out a *subset* of hallucinations it calls **confabulations**: cases where the model fluently makes claims that are both wrong **and arbitrary**, meaning the answer is sensitive to irrelevant details such as the random seed. Their example is a medical question, "What is the target of Sotorasib?", answered sometimes as KRAS-G12'C' (correct) and sometimes KRAS-G12'D' (incorrect) under identical instructions. They explicitly distinguish this from three other mechanisms with the same symptom: being consistently wrong because of erroneous training data such as common misconceptions; the model "lying" in pursuit of a reward; and systematic failures of reasoning or generalisation. They argue that lumping these mechanisms together under "hallucination" is unhelpful, and they state outright that their method **does not guarantee factuality because it does not help when LLM outputs are systematically bad**. Semantic entropy is thus recast as a *seed-variation consistency check on meaning*, and its target class of error is defined by that mechanism.

### Method changes

The core three steps are unchanged and the derivation is the same one, with three substantive additions.

1. **A discrete (black-box) estimator.** Where token probabilities are unavailable, `P(C_i|x)` is approximated by the proportion of sampled answers falling in each cluster — that is, every generated output is treated as equally probable, the empirical categorical distribution. In the limit of `M` this converges to the standard estimator by the law of large numbers, and empirically it performs similarly. This is what lets them run GPT-4, which at the time exposed neither output probabilities nor hidden states.
2. **An explicit normalisation in the main estimator.** Equation (5) is a Rao-Blackwellized Monte Carlo integration with `P(C_i|x) = P(c_i|x) / Σ_c P(c|x)`, so cluster probabilities form a proper categorical distribution. They note the reason: without this normalisation, length normalisation can push cluster "probabilities" above one, producing degeneracies. The ICLR estimator (equation 4 there) had no such normalisation step.
3. **A decomposition pipeline for paragraph-length text.** Semantic entropy is natural for a sentence expressing one proposition, and awkward for a paragraph expressing many that may only partly agree. Their solution is indirect and worth stating in full, because naive resampling of a sentence varies *which aspect* of a biography gets described rather than the model's confidence in any fact: decompose the paragraph into factual claims with an LLM; for each claim, have an LLM construct questions that claim might have answered (six, from two prompts of three, for coverage); sample `M = 3` new answers per question from the original model; compute semantic entropy over those answers *plus the original claim*, which keeps the estimator anchored to the claim and catches questions that were reinterpreted; then average the per-question entropies into one score per claim. They name step 2, question generation, as the main source of errors in the procedure. A refusal heuristic is added for GPT-4: if more than half the answers contain 'not available', 'not provided', 'unknown' or 'unclear', semantic uncertainty is set to maximal.

Also changed: **entailment can now be judged by a general-purpose LLM**, prompted directly ("Does Possible Answer 1 semantically entail Possible Answer 2? Respond with entailment, contradiction, or neutral"), not only by DeBERTa-large-MNLI. For sentence-length experiments they settle on **GPT-3.5**, on the grounds that its entailment predictions agree well with human raters and give good detection performance. For the paragraph-length setting they revert to DeBERTa and, because DeBERTa has slightly lower recall, relax the criterion to a "non-defeating" check — the answers count as sharing meaning if at least one entails the other and neither contradicts. Sampling also changes: temperature 1 with nucleus sampling (P = 0.9) and top-K (K = 50), varying only the random seed, with a separate low-temperature (0.1) generation standing in for the model's "best" answer; they report the method is not particularly sensitive to sampling-scheme details. `M = 10` generations is retained for sentence-length work.

### Scale, tasks, and models

Far broader than the ICLR paper. **Datasets:** TriviaQA (trivia), SQuAD 1.1 (reading comprehension, with v2.0's deliberately unanswerable questions excluded), BioASQ 11B (life sciences), NQ-Open (real Google Search queries), SVAMP (elementary-school maths word problems), plus **FactualBio**, a new biography dataset released with the paper. 400 train and 400 test examples per dataset. Context passages are deliberately withheld, because with context provided accuracy is too high for current models to show confabulations. **Models:** LLaMA 2 Chat (7B, 13B, 70B), Falcon Instruct (7B, 40B), Mistral Instruct (7B) for sentence-length answers, and GPT-4 (v.0613) for FactualBio. That is **30 task-model combinations**. Answers are sentence-length (96 ± 70 characters), with a short-phrase setting in the Supplementary Information. Correctness labelling is upgraded: GPT-4 judges whether the proposed answer means the same as the reference, since Rouge-style overlap fails when a sentence-length answer is compared to a short reference. FactualBio consists of 21 individuals sampled from WikiBio, 150 extracted factual claims, hand-labelled, of which 45 are incorrect.

### New metric: AURAC, and the abstention framing

Alongside AUROC, they introduce **rejection accuracy at X%** — the model's accuracy on the most-confident X% of inputs — and summarise it as the **AURAC**, the area under that curve. This is the addition that most directly concerns the Subject, because it evaluates semantic entropy *as an abstention mechanism* rather than only as a diagnostic: the score is used to refuse the questions most likely to produce confabulations, and AURAC measures the accuracy improvement a user would actually experience. They note AURAC is more sensitive to the model's overall accuracy than AUROC is.

### Headline results

- Averaged over the **30 task-model combinations**, semantic entropy reaches **AUROC 0.790**, against **naive (length-normalised) entropy 0.691**, **`p(True)` 0.698**, and a **supervised embedding-regression baseline 0.687**.
- Performance is stable at **0.78 to 0.81 AUROC across model families (LLaMA, Falcon, Mistral) and scales (7B to 70B)**.
- The discrete, probability-free variant performs similarly to the standard estimator.
- The supervised embedding-regression baseline (a logistic regression on the final hidden state, inspired by Kadavath et al.'s P(IK)) does well in-distribution and **deteriorates off-distribution**, which the authors stress is the case that matters most, since reliable uncertainty is most needed when the distribution shifts. `p(True)`, supervised in-context with up to 20 few-shot examples, is also beaten — though they observe it **improves with model size** and might become competitive for very capable honest models in settings the model understands well, which they add "are, however, not the most important cases to have good uncertainty."
- On paragraph-length FactualBio with GPT-4, discrete semantic entropy beats both a plain **self-check** baseline (asking the model whether the statement is likely true) and a paragraph-adapted `p(True)`, on both AUROC and AURAC. One honest exception: `p(True)` gains a narrow edge on rejection accuracy once more than 20% of answers are rejected.
- They read the whole result as evidence that **confabulations are a principal category of error** in current generations: the supervised baselines can catch other error types too and are still beaten.
- A memorable line from the Discussion: the success of semantic entropy suggests LLMs are better at "knowing what they don't know" than previously argued — "they just don't know they know what they don't know." The self-check baseline ought to work well if models had good self-knowledge, and semantic entropy is much better than it.

### Stated limitations of the Nature paper

- It detects **confabulations only**. It explicitly does not address models that are **confidently wrong** through systematically bad training objectives, systematic reasoning errors, or deliberate misleading of the user. Those are held to be different mechanisms requiring separate handling.
- **Clustering can be over-sensitive.** Their own Table 1 includes a case where semantic entropy is wrongly high because the clustering separated answers giving a precise date from answers giving only the year — a distinction that mattered not at all in that context. They draw two lessons: context and judgement matter in clustering, and evaluating against fixed reference answers fails to capture the open-ended flexibility of real conversational deployment.
- **The paragraph pipeline's weak link is question generation.** Generated questions are "not necessarily well-targeted" and sometimes miss obvious aspects of a fact; this is named as the main error source.
- **Semantic equivalence is awkward for long passages** expressing many propositions that may agree only partially — hence the decomposition workaround.
- **No standard errors are reported** for the main figure. Their stated reason is that the distributional characteristics are more a property of the chosen models and datasets than of the method, so consistency of relative results across datasets is treated as the stronger indicator.
- Aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty are **not separated**; they say advances that separate them would extend the approach beyond entropy.
- Practical constraint on a baseline: `p(True)` cannot always fit 20 few-shot examples in context when questions are long or many generations are used, so the shot count is sometimes reduced.

### Continuity to note

Both papers share the same core equations, the same Paris example, the same bidirectional-entailment definition, the same context-dependence argument, the same length-normalisation reasoning, and the same three-step algorithm. The Nature paper is best read as the ICLR method scaled up, given a black-box variant, extended from a single sentence to decomposed paragraphs, framed around a sharper error class, and evaluated as an abstention policy rather than only as a score.

## Open questions the papers themselves raise

From the ICLR paper:

- **Better clustering algorithms.** Their own equivalence relation is "soft", therefore not strictly transitive, therefore the equivalence classes are not strictly unique and can depend on comparison order. They name this as an area for improvement.
- **Unequal token importance is only partially solved.** Merging semantically identical generations reduces the influence of unimportant tokens, but they say they do not demonstrate this empirically, and that semantic entropy "will still pay too much attention to non-keyword likelihoods." They suggest supervised, language-model-based uncertainty tools might handle this better — an unusual concession given the paper's overall position.
- **Sampling hyperparameters beyond temperature.** They study temperature and leave top-p, top-k, and the wider design space to future work while arguing these choices have been overlooked.
- **Beyond question answering.** They hope semantic equivalence can carry over to settings like summarisation, but say progress on paraphrase identification in those settings is likely required first.
- **Beyond entropy.** Semantic likelihoods could be extended to other probabilistic tools such as mutual information.
- **Deception is out of scope.** They state plainly that the method does not protect against models capable of deception, as opposed to models merely uncertain among several meaningful options, and offer the combination of model internals with model predictions as the hope of staying ahead of model capabilities.
- **Calibration in generative QA is a problematic evaluation** for the reasons given above; what should replace it is left open.

From the Nature paper: separating aleatoric from epistemic uncertainty; algorithmic adaptation to other domains such as abstractive summarisation; extension to other input variations such as rephrasing or counterfactual scenarios, which they suggest could make the method a form of **cross-examination** for scalable oversight through debate; improving the question-generation step; and clustering that is sensitive to which distinctions matter in context.

## Appreciation

**Mechanism — strong, and clean enough to reason about.** The central observation is one of those that seems obvious once stated and was evidently not: a language model's token-level entropy conflates two different uncertainties, one about *what to say* and one about *how to say it*, and only the first is decision-relevant. The repair is well matched to the diagnosis. Because a semantic equivalence relation partitions the sequence space into classes, and because probability mass is additive over a partition, summing sequence probabilities within a meaning-cluster is the mathematically correct way to obtain the probability of a meaning — no approximation is being smuggled in at that step. Bidirectional entailment is a principled operationalisation with real lineage in linguistics and NLP, and the insistence on judging entailment *relative to the context* is the kind of detail that distinguishes a working method from a plausible one. Three further mechanistic strengths deserve credit. The method needs no labels, no fine-tuning, and no architectural change, so it can be bolted onto a model you did not train. Table 2 supplies a causal story rather than only a correlation — the model fragments across more distinct meanings precisely when it is about to be wrong — and that story is independently checkable. And Table 8's margin-probability analysis is a genuinely diagnostic experiment: it identifies a competing method's failure and shows the failure has exactly the shape the thesis predicts.

I also credit the paper's methodological honesty in two specific places. It strengthens its own baselines rather than inheriting weakened ones, explicitly pointing out that prior work's temperature of 1.0 disadvantages predictive entropy, and it validates every soft component it depends on — the entailment classifier against 300 hand-labelled pairs, the correctness metric against 200 hand-labelled answers, and the high-temperature failure against 100 hand-labelled classifications.

**Magnitude — real but modest, and this should be kept separate from the above.** The gains over the strongest baseline are small in absolute terms: roughly 0.83 against 0.80 AUROC on TriviaQA, and 0.77 against 0.75 on CoQA. The Nature paper's 30-combination average, 0.790 against 0.691, looks larger, but part of that distance comes from a naive-entropy baseline in a harder no-context setting rather than from the same head-to-head as the ICLR comparison. An AUROC near 0.8 is a useful signal and nowhere near a reliable gate: it means a randomly chosen wrong answer scores higher than a randomly chosen right one about four times in five, which at any single operating threshold still leaves substantial errors on both sides. The gap over `p(True)` is the large one, and that comparison is the most load-bearing for the Subject, though it is also the comparison most weakened by the compute constraints the authors disclose (30B open model, 10-shot, against the original's 52B proprietary model at 20-shot). Two magnitudes are more impressive than the headline AUROC. First, robustness: 0.78 to 0.81 across three model families and a 10x range of scale, with no per-task tuning, is a stronger claim than any single number. Second, cost: 10 samples plus pairwise comparisons from a 1.5B NLI model is a cheap price for the signal, and the discrete variant removes even the requirement of logprob access.

## How it could serve a harness-design effort, with limitations and concerns

**What is directly usable.** Semantic entropy is a per-generation, unsupervised, model-agnostic score for "do not trust this output," computable from ten samples plus a small entailment model, requiring no labels and — in the discrete variant — not even token probabilities. For a harness that must decide whether to accept a step's output, that is a cheap instrument that can be attached to any model, including one behind an API. The Nature paper takes the step that matters most for a harness by evaluating the score as an **abstention policy** (rejection accuracy and AURAC) rather than only as a diagnostic, and shows the resulting accuracy improvement on the retained questions is real. The paragraph pipeline is also, structurally, a *verification decomposition* recipe: break a long output into propositions, generate the questions each proposition would answer, re-sample answers, and score consistency per proposition. A harness needing per-claim rather than per-response confidence could reuse that shape. And there is a striking cheap fallback: the bare **count of distinct meaning-clusters** already reaches 0.79 AUROC on TriviaQA with no probabilities and no entropy arithmetic at all.

**The core limitation for the Subject, stated plainly.** This is a signal about **one finished generation answering one question**. Nothing in either paper addresses a multi-step trajectory, a loop, a stopping criterion, progress detection, or a hand-off decision. The unit of analysis throughout is a single question with a single reference answer. The closest either paper comes to a sequential setting is the paragraph decomposition, and that is a *post hoc* decomposition of one completed output, not a judgement made during an unfolding process. To use semantic entropy in a loop, a harness designer must supply everything that makes it a loop signal: what the resampled unit is when the "question" is an action or a plan rather than a fact; how per-step scores aggregate over a trajectory; what threshold triggers escalation as against retry; and whether entropy trending flat across iterations means converged or stuck. None of that is in evidence here, and the papers do not claim it is.

**The confidently-wrong case is explicitly not covered — and this is the deepest concern.** The method measures *disagreement among samples about meaning*. When every sample agrees on the same wrong answer, semantic entropy is low and reads as confident. The Nature paper says so directly: it detects confabulations, defined by arbitrariness, and does not help when outputs are systematically bad, when the model has absorbed a common misconception, when it is pursuing a reward, or when it makes a systematic reasoning error. The ICLR paper adds that the method does not protect against deception. For an agent loop this is the failure mode with the worst consequences, because a consistent wrong belief is exactly what a self-consistency check cannot see and exactly what makes a loop terminate early with a confident bad result. Semantic entropy must therefore be treated as one channel among several — it can never be the sole gate, and it needs pairing with an external check whose evidence is independent of the model's own sampling distribution, such as a test or a verifier.

**Further concerns a harness designer should hold onto.**

1. **The signal degrades exactly where output quality degrades.** At T = 1.5 the entailment classifier falls to 61% accuracy and semantic entropy loses to the simpler baseline. When generations become malformed, the clustering step — the whole basis of the method — stops working. A harness cannot assume the uncertainty channel stays trustworthy while the generation channel is failing; degraded output means degraded measurement.
2. **Uncertainty quality is now a function of decoding configuration.** The optimum was 0.5 on both datasets and beam search actively hurt. A harness that tunes temperature for task performance, or uses beam search, may silently lose the uncertainty signal, since the setting that maximises accuracy is not the setting that maximises AUROC. Sampling policy and monitoring policy are coupled, and the paper's own numbers show that changing one changes the other.
3. **Cluster granularity is a judgement call with no principled setting.** The equivalence relation is soft and not truly transitive, so clusters can depend on comparison order; the Nature paper's date-versus-year example shows over-sensitive clustering inflating uncertainty on a distinction that did not matter in context. In an agent setting, where two phrasings of an action may be equivalent for one purpose and importantly different for another, "what counts as the same meaning" becomes a design decision the harness has to make, not something the NLI model can settle.
4. **The compute multiple is real but bounded.** Ten samples per judged output plus pairwise NLI comparisons is roughly a 10x generation cost with a smaller clustering overhead — tolerable at one decision point, expensive at every step of a long loop. The paper's own evidence that `M = 10` suffices, and that the bare cluster count is a usable score, gives two ways to buy the signal more cheaply.
5. **Validation is not adversarial.** Correctness labels come from fuzzy string match (ICLR) or a GPT-4 judge (Nature), against fixed reference answers on standard QA datasets, and the Nature paper explicitly notes that fixed references do not capture the open-ended flexibility of real deployments. No significance testing is reported in either paper. The evidence is a consistent direction of effect over many conditions, which is genuinely persuasive for *ranking methods* and thin for *predicting the numbers a specific deployment will see*.
6. **A dependency to notice.** Where a general LLM is used as the entailment judge (GPT-3.5 in the Nature sentence-length experiments), the method's independence from model self-report is partially reduced — an LLM is now in the measurement loop, even though it judges pairwise entailment rather than truth, which is a much easier and better-posed question than "is my answer right?"

**On the `p(True)` comparison, which is the finding most worth carrying forward.** Across both papers, asking the model to judge its own answer performed worse than measuring the spread of its samples — dramatically worse in the ICLR results, and worse on average across 30 combinations in Nature, where it also lost to the paragraph-level self-check. Two qualifications belong with that. The Nature authors observe that `p(True)` improves with model size and could become competitive for very capable honest models on tasks they understand well, and they add that those are not the cases where good uncertainty matters most. Their formulation of the underlying situation is the sharpest sentence in either paper for the Subject: models are better at knowing what they don't know than had been argued — "they just don't know they know what they don't know." The information is present in the sampling distribution; asking the model to introspect is a poor way to extract it.

## Five citations worth chasing next

1. **Kadavath et al., 2022, "Language models (mostly) know what they know" (arXiv:2207.05221).** The direct antagonist of both papers and the source of `p(True)` and P(IK). It is the reference point for self-evaluation as a confidence signal and is cited on nine pages of the ICLR paper.
2. **Malinin & Gales, 2020, "Uncertainty estimation in autoregressive structured prediction" (arXiv:2002.07650).** The origin of the length-normalised predictive-entropy baseline and of ensemble-based NLG uncertainty. Needed to understand what semantic entropy is improving on and what the ensemble version would add.
3. **Lin, Hilton & Evans, 2022, "Teaching models to express their uncertainty in words" (arXiv:2205.14334).** The supervised, verbalised-confidence alternative — the other main way to get a confidence number out of a model, and the approach whose distribution-shift fragility both papers use to motivate going unsupervised.
4. **Jiang, Araki, Ding & Neubig, 2021, "How can we know when language models know? On the calibration of language models for question answering" (TACL 9:962-977).** The calibration counterpart, and the source of the finding that log-likelihood correlates only weakly with correctness in generative QA — the empirical gap semantic entropy is designed to close.
5. **He et al., 2020, "DeBERTa: Decoding-enhanced BERT with disentangled attention" (arXiv:2006.03654), together with Williams, Nangia & Bowman, 2017, MNLI (arXiv:1704.05426).** The entailment engine the whole method rests on. Since the ICLR paper's failure mode at high temperature is the NLI classifier collapsing to 61% accuracy, the properties and limits of this model are load-bearing for anyone deploying the method.

Worth noting alongside these, from the Nature paper's own reference list, that its scalable-oversight framing points at cross-examination and debate as the intended destination for this line of work.
