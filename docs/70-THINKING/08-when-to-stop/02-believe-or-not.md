# To Believe or Not to Believe Your LLM

## Density

**Density: HIGH** — Almost every page carries load-bearing content (a definition, a theorem, an algorithm, or an experiment), there is very little restatement, and the core contribution is directly about a system deciding whether its own output is trustworthy enough to serve or should be abstained from, which sits squarely inside the Subject.

## Name, keywords, year, venue

**Name.** Yasin Abbasi Yadkori, Ilja Kuzborskij, András György, Csaba Szepesvári, "To Believe or Not to Believe Your LLM: Iterative Prompting for Estimating Epistemic Uncertainty."

**Year / venue.** arXiv v1 4 June 2024, v2 17 July 2024 (arXiv:2406.02543, cs.LG, CC BY 4.0). Published in *Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 37* (NeurIPS 2024); OpenReview `k6iyUfwdI9`. Peer-reviewed, as the brief states. I read arXiv v2 in full (main text plus appendices A–E).

**Keywords (mine).** Epistemic versus aleatoric uncertainty; mutual information as a hallucination score; pseudo joint distribution; iterative prompting; ground-truth independence assumption; missing mass; abstention policy; semantic clustering; multi-label queries; second-order uncertainty.

## Approach

The paper attacks a specific hole in existing uncertainty quantification for LLMs. Standard scores — log-likelihood of the greedy response, or the (semantic) entropy of a sample of responses — measure *total* uncertainty. Total uncertainty conflates two things the paper insists on separating: **epistemic** uncertainty, which comes from the model not knowing the ground truth (facts, language), and **aleatoric** uncertainty, which is irreducible because the query genuinely admits several correct answers ("Name a city in the UK"). The authors point out that a perfect predictor on a multi-answer question and a useless predictor on a single-answer question can have exactly the same total uncertainty, so any first-order score must either abstain wrongly on legitimate multi-answer queries or fail to flag hallucinations on them. Their stated goal is deliberately asymmetric: they do not try to certify that epistemic uncertainty is *small*; they try to detect when epistemic uncertainty *alone* is large, which is a lower bound rather than an upper bound, and which is the case in which the output should not be believed.

The mechanism rests on one observation. If several answers to the same query are drawn from the ground truth, they should be mutually independent — the joint distribution over multiple ground-truth answers must be a product distribution. An LLM, being sequential, can be made to produce a *joint* distribution over several answers by iterative prompting: ask for an answer, then ask again with the first answer embedded in the prompt, then again with the first two, and so on, multiplying the conditionals by the chain rule. If the model is confident in what it knows, its later answers are insensitive to the earlier answers pasted into its context and the joint is near-product; if the model is unsure, the pasted-in answers drag the next answer around, the joint is far from product, and the dependence is visible as mutual information. Section 3 demonstrates the amplification effect empirically and Section 3.1 gives a mechanistic sketch of it in a single attention head.

**The prompt family.** The prompt function `F_t(x, Y_1..Y_t)` is fixed and literal:

> Consider the following question:
> Q: *x*
> One answer to question Q is *Y_1*. Another answer to question Q is *Y_2*. […] Another answer to question Q is *Y_t*.
> Provide an answer to the following question:
> Q: *x*. A:

**Assumption 4.1 (ground-truth independence).** `P(Y_t | F_{t-1}(x, Y_1..Y_{t-1})) = P(Y_t | x)` for all t and all strings. The authors are explicit that this assumption is a property of *this prompt construction*, not of language in general: because the previous responses are embedded as complete alternative answers rather than as a continuing text, the ground truth is entitled to ignore them. They note it would fail if the `Y_i` were partial answers — a step of an algorithm, a part of a story — because then the next output genuinely depends on the earlier ones.

**Definition 4.2 (pseudo joint distribution).** `Q̃(Y_1..Y_n | x) = Q(Y_1 | F_0(x)) · Q(Y_2 | F_1(x,Y_1)) ··· Q(Y_n | F_{n-1}(x,Y_1..Y_{n-1}))`. It is called *pseudo* because ordinary chain-rule conditioning is replaced by conditioning on a prompt-function of the earlier variables. Sampling from `Q̃` is exactly the iterative prompting loop.

**Definition 4.4 (the epistemic uncertainty metric).** For query x, epistemic uncertainty is `D_KL(Q̃, P̃)` — the KL divergence from the model's pseudo joint to the ground-truth pseudo joint, in that order. That direction is chosen deliberately: it is the direction that penalises `Q̃` for putting large mass where `P̃` puts small mass, which is precisely a hallucination. The reverse order `D_KL(P̃, Q̃)` would be a conditional excess risk under log loss, which the authors say is a much harder object to control because it requires bounding generalisation, estimation and approximation error together.

**Theorem 4.5 (the computable lower bound).** For every `P̃` satisfying Assumption 4.1, `D_KL(Q̃, P̃) ≥ I(Q̃)`, where `I(Q̃) = D_KL(Q̃, Q̃^⊗)` is the mutual information of the model-derived joint against the product of its own marginals. This is the load-bearing result. The bound holds *uniformly over all admissible ground truths* and depends only on `Q̃`, which the practitioner can sample; the authors say this uniformity is why they chose `D_KL(Q̃, P̃)` as the metric out of the many available divergences. The proof (Appendix D) is three lines of algebra: expand the KL as `−H(Q̃) +` cross-entropy, use Assumption 4.1 to replace `P(y_i | F_{i-1}(·))` by `P(y_i)` so the ground-truth joint factorises, then lower-bound the cross-entropy term against `P` by the corresponding term against `Q̃`'s own marginals using entropy ≤ cross-entropy. What remains is exactly the mutual information. Note that `Q̃`'s "marginals" here are the marginals of the pseudo joint, and the paper is careful to say these are *not* equal to `Q(Y_i)` in general, because Assumption 4.1 almost never holds for `Q` — that gap is the signal.

**How the separation of epistemic from aleatoric works.** Aleatoric uncertainty is a property of the ground truth `P`. Under Assumption 4.1 it enters `P̃` only through the product of identical marginals, so it inflates the *entropy* of both `P̃` and `Q̃` without creating dependence between coordinates. Mutual information is zero for any product distribution regardless of how spread out the marginals are; it responds only to the coordinates influencing each other, which under this prompt design can only happen if the model is being swayed by its own previous answers rather than by knowledge in its weights. So a model that genuinely knows five correct answers has high entropy and near-zero MI, whereas a model that is guessing has whatever entropy plus non-zero MI. That is the whole separation argument, and it is an argument about the metric, not an empirical claim.

**The unreliability condition, precisely.** The output is declared unreliable when the estimated mutual information of the iteratively-prompted pseudo joint exceeds a calibrated threshold: the abstention policy is `a_λ(x) = 1` (abstain) if `Î_k(γ1, γ2, x) ≥ λ`, and `0` (serve a response) otherwise, with `λ` tuned on a held-out calibration sample for the task at hand. The chain of justification is: a large `Î_k` certifies (up to the estimation error of Theorem 4.6, and given Assumption 4.1) that `I(Q̃)` is large, which lower-bounds `D_KL(Q̃, P̃)`, which means the model's joint cannot be close to *any* admissible ground truth, and since MI is blind to aleatoric spread, that distance must be epistemic. High MI therefore means "only epistemic uncertainty is large", hence hallucination. The converse is not claimed: a small `Î_k` certifies nothing, because a lower bound on the divergence being small does not make the divergence small.

**Estimation (Section 4.1, Algorithm 1).** `I(Q̃)` requires evaluating `Q̃` over a potentially infinite support, so it is replaced by a finite-sample plug-in. Draw k tuples from `Q̃`, keep the unique ones (index set U), renormalise the model's own probabilities over U to get `μ̂` and build `μ̂^⊗` from the coordinate-wise sums over U, then compute `Î_k(γ1,γ2) = Σ_{i∈U} μ̂(X_i) ln((μ̂(X_i)+γ1)/(μ̂^⊗(X_i)+γ2))`. The stabilisers `γ1, γ2 ≥ 0` (typically 1/k) stand in for the probability mass never sampled and keep the estimate bounded — the authors relate this to universal coding, Laplace smoothing and Good–Turing smoothing.

**Theorem 4.6 (finite-sample error).** With `X` finite, `γ1 = 1/(k|X^n|)` and `γ2 ≥ γ1 + n(1−Z)`, then with probability at least 1−δ, `I(μ) ≥ (1−ε_k) Î_k(γ1,γ2) − (1/k + (1 + n ln(1+k|X|)) ε_k)` where `ε_k = E[U_k] + sqrt(ln(1/δ)/k)` and `U_k` is the missing mass. A second, "optimistic" version replaces the full support by an effective support `X̃` with `μ(X̃) ≥ 1−δ_supp`, paying only `ln(1+k|X̃|)` instead of `n ln(1+k|X|)` at the cost of an extra `δ_supp` term — an exponential-in-n improvement when the effective support is polynomial in n. The error is governed by the expected missing mass, whose worst-case decay is slow (`E[U_k] ≤ h/ln k` for entropy-bounded countable distributions), but which the authors argue is fast in practice because language is Zipfian: for a Zipf tail with exponent α > 1 they show `E[U_k] = O(k^{−((α−1)/α − β)})`, which for α = 2 approaches the `1/√k` concentration rate. Appendix E.4 adds a data-dependent estimate of `E[U_k]` on the QA benchmarks and reports that the per-question upper bounds concentrate near zero, with effective support rarely exceeding roughly 100 strings at 95% mass.

**Semantic equivalence (Section 4.2, Algorithm 2).** The lexical bound is loose because different strings can mean the same thing. A similarity function `s` with threshold `τ` is assumed to induce a clustering of the string space; probabilities are summed within clusters to give `Q'` and `P'`, and Theorems 4.5 and 4.6 are applied to the clustered distributions. The paper notes `I(μ) ≥ I(μ')` by the data-processing/monotonicity property of KL, so the clustered quantity is the tighter and more meaningful one to threshold.

**The variant actually used in the experiments (Algorithm 3).** For the experiments the estimator is restructured to exploit the LLM's log-likelihood function, and it takes `Q` (not the pseudo joint) as input. Draw k = 10 responses at temperature 0.9, deduplicate, cluster by similarity to get cluster centres S, then for each response Y compute the probability of every generated response under the prompt `F_1(x, Y)`. Build `μ̂_1(X_i)` from the aggregated first-round probabilities and `μ̂_2(X_t | X_i)` from the aggregated second-round ones, set `μ̂(X_i,X_t) = μ̂_1(X_i) μ̂_2(X_t|X_i)` and `μ̂^⊗(X_i,X_t) = μ̂_1(X_i) Σ_j μ̂_1(X_j) μ̂_2(X_t|X_j)`, then take the same log-ratio sum over pairs. The paper states plainly that **n = 2 is sufficient** for effective hallucination detection on the benchmarks considered, and the experiments use `n = 2`, `k = 10`, `γ1 = γ2 = 0` (the zero stabilisers being an approximation to the very small theoretical values appropriate when the response set is large).

**Call cost.** The depth of the iterative prompting in the experiments is two: an initial round and one feedback round. The concrete budget per query is k = 10 sampled generations at temperature 0.9 (plus a greedy generation for the T0 baseline), followed by a scoring pass in which, for each of the |S| cluster representatives Y, the log-probabilities of all the generated responses are evaluated under the prompt `F_1(x, Y)` — that is, on the order of |S| extra conditioned prompts, each scoring |S| candidate continuations, so roughly quadratic in the number of distinct clustered answers but requiring no extra *sampling* beyond the first 10. This makes the method strictly more expensive than greedy log-likelihood (1 call) and modestly more expensive than semantic entropy (which needs the same 10 samples but no second-round conditioned scoring). The method needs no extra parameters, no training change, and no access to internal states — only sampling and log-probabilities. The authors separately note that using LLM self-prompting for the text comparison instead of F1 matching would give more reliable clustering, particularly for long outputs, but is "computationally much more expensive"; they did not do it.

## Models targeted and benchmarks

**Model.** A single model: **Gemini 1.0 Pro**, used both to generate outputs and to produce the scores. No second model, no ablation across model families or sizes.

**Datasets.** Three bases plus mixtures.
- **TriviaQA** (Joshi et al. 2017): a random subset of 50,000 datapoints; 38,870 survive filtering. Predominantly single-label.
- **AmbigQA** (Min et al. 2020): the entire dataset, 12,038 datapoints; 5,315 survive filtering. Predominantly single-label; it does contain some multi-label queries, but the authors inspected them and found the LLM has low entropy on most, so they behave as single-label in practice.
- **WordNet-derived multi-label set** (built by the authors from Fellbaum 1998): all 6,015 datapoints at depth ≥ 4 of the `physical_entity` subtree, turned into queries "Name a type of *entity*." with the WordNet children as target labels; 3,296 survive filtering, and 842 survive the further filter of output entropy > 0.7 used for the mixed-dataset experiments.
- **TriviaQA+WordNet** and **AmbigQA+WordNet**: 842 high-entropy WordNet points combined with 842 randomly chosen points from the respective QA set. These mixtures are where the paper's claimed advantage lives.

All tasks are **closed-book open-domain question answering with short answers**. A filter keeps only queries whose greedy response and at least one sampled response are under 20 characters, on the stated grounds that F1 matching and log-probabilities are less reliable for long sequences. Nothing multi-step, agentic, or long-form is evaluated.

**Baselines.** Four scoring methods are compared, all feeding the same abstention-policy shape.
- **T0** — probability of the greedy (temperature-zero) response; the naive log-likelihood threshold. Default served answer: the greedy response.
- **S.E.** — the semantic-entropy method of Kuhn et al. (2023): entropy over k = 10 samples after aggregating probabilities of equivalent responses and renormalising. Default served answer: the highest aggregate-probability sampled response.
- **S.V.** — a version of the self-verification method of Kadavath et al. (2022): take the highest-aggregate-probability answer `Y_1`, then ask the model "Consider the following question: Q: *x*. One answer to question Q is *Y_1*. Is the above answer to question Q correct? Answer True or False. A:" and score the normalised probability of the "True" token. The authors include it deliberately as a score that, unlike the first-order ones, should not be sensitive to the size of the label set.
- **M.I.** — the proposed mutual information score, k = 10, n = 2, γ1 = γ2 = 0.

T0 and S.E. are described as **first-order** because they only look at the marginal distribution of a single response; the proposed score is second-order in that it looks at a joint over several.

## Author incentive

All four authors are at **Google DeepMind** (the paper carries a single affiliation line and no separate funding statement). The evaluation model is Gemini 1.0 Pro, DeepMind's own model, and the paper's stated motivation cites the Gemini technical report for the existence of hallucinations. The relevant stakes are institutional rather than commercial in a narrow sense: the work advertises a hallucination-abstention method for the authors' own model family, and it builds directly on a prior paper by an overlapping author set (Yadkori et al. 2024, "Mitigating LLM hallucinations via conformal abstention"), which is cited both as the source of the calibration procedure and as a first-order baseline family the present work improves on. The authors are established learning-theory researchers (Szepesvári, György), and the paper's centre of gravity — a uniform lower bound, a missing-mass analysis, non-asymptotic constants — reflects that; the theory is the product being sold, and the experiments are comparatively modest. There is no code release announced in the text beyond Python implementations of Algorithms 2 and 3 printed in Appendices A and B.

## Measurement methodology (brushed)

**Dependent variables.** A precision-recall trade-off defined for abstention: **recall** is the percentage of queries on which the method does *not* abstain, and **precision** is the percentage of correct decisions among those served (the figures often plot **error rate** = 1 − precision). Correctness of a served answer is decided by **F1 token-overlap against the target, thresholded at 0.25** — the same similarity function used for semantic clustering, with τ = 0.25.

**What is controlled.** The four methods share the same model, the same k = 10 samples at temperature 0.9, the same clustering rule and the same abstention-policy form, so the only thing varying is the score. Every method also has its own defined "default answer" served when it does not abstain, which is not identical across methods (T0 serves the greedy answer, S.E. and S.V. serve the top aggregated sample, M.I. serves the highest-probability sample under the marginalised pseudo joint), so the comparison is of complete policies rather than of scores alone.

**Two evaluation regimes.** (i) Sweep all abstention thresholds and plot the whole PR curve (Figure 5). (ii) Fix a target loss rate, calibrate the threshold on a calibration split, and measure error and recall on a disjoint test split (Figure 6). Regime (ii) uses 500 random WordNet points plus 500 random TriviaQA points for calibration and another such draw for test, with target loss rates of 0.05 for TriviaQA and 0.15 for AmbigQA.

**N and significance.** Dataset sizes after filtering are large (38,870 / 5,315 / 3,296), but the headline mixed-dataset experiments run on 842 + 842 points, and the calibrated experiment on 500 + 500 per split. Significance is treated lightly but not absently: the calibrated experiment is **repeated 10 times**, and the paper reports means with 95% / 2-standard-deviation confidence intervals under a Gaussian approximation. The PR-curve figures (Figure 5) carry no error bars and no repetitions. There is no significance test between methods, no multiple-comparison correction, and no seed or variance reporting for the single-model generation step.

**Notable methodological choices to flag.** The multi-label WordNet dataset is constructed by the authors, and the mixture experiments additionally *filter it* to entropy > 0.7 to "enhance the intended effect" — the authors state this openly, but it means the headline comparison is run on a distribution deliberately enriched for the case the method is designed to win. The F1-at-0.25 match function serves simultaneously as the semantic-equivalence clustering rule and as the correctness judge, so clustering error and grading error are correlated. The 20-character length filter removes exactly the long-form outputs on which the authors admit their scoring machinery is least reliable.

## Key findings

1. **Probability amplification by iterative prompting (Section 3, Figures 1–3).** Repeating a candidate answer in the prompt shifts the model's output, and the size of the shift tracks the model's epistemic confidence. On queries where the model is confident and correct ("What is the capital of the UK?", "Who was the first US president?"), repeating a wrong answer up to 100 times only pushes the conditional normalised probability of the correct answer from ≈1.0 down to about 96% — the correct answer survives. On queries where the model is genuinely unsure ("What is the national instrument of Ireland?", harp 0.936 vs Uilleann pipes 0.063; "Which actor became M in Skyfall?", Ralph Fiennes 0.651 vs Judi Dench 0.348), the probability of the correct answer "quickly collapses to near zero" under the same treatment. On genuinely multi-label queries ("Name a city in the UK", "Name a yellow fruit"), putting one correct answer in the prompt leaves the other correct answers with relatively large probability. These three behaviours are the empirical basis for believing that sensitivity-to-own-previous-answers separates the two uncertainty types.

2. **A mechanistic account of that sensitivity in one attention head (Section 3.1).** In an idealised single self-attention head over statement-level feature vectors, if the query X lies in the span of the large principal components of the key–query product `E^T W^Q (W^K)^T`, the softmax is dominated by X and the output follows the in-weight knowledge stored in `W^V`, giving `f(Z) ≈ P(· | X)`. If X has little overlap with those components — the case for a query seen rarely in training — the repeated in-context element Y dominates and gets copied. Even when X *is* in the span, repeating Y t times multiplies its total softmax weight by t and can overwhelm X for large enough t. This is offered as an explanation of why in-context copying is a signal of low in-weight confidence; it is an analysis of an idealised head, not a measurement on a real transformer.

3. **The bound itself (Theorem 4.5).** Mutual information of the iteratively-prompted pseudo joint is a valid lower bound on the epistemic uncertainty metric, uniformly over all ground truths satisfying the independence assumption, and computable from model outputs alone.

4. **Estimation is tractable in practice despite an infinite support (Theorem 4.6 plus Appendix E).** The finite-sample MI estimator's error is controlled by the expected missing mass, which is provably slow-decaying in the worst case but fast under a Zipf tail, and which the authors measure to be near zero on their QA benchmarks, with effective support rarely above ~100 strings at 95% mass. This is the sense in which the paper's contribution (ii) claims "negligible error even though LLMs' derived joint distributions are defined over potentially infinite supports".

5. **On predominantly single-label data, M.I. ties with semantic entropy and beats the naive and self-verification baselines (Figure 5ab).** On TriviaQA and AmbigQA, M.I. > T0 and M.I. > S.V., but M.I. ≈ S.E. The authors read this straightforwardly as expected and not as a win: on single-label data a first-order score is sufficient, and even AmbigQA's multi-label queries turn out to have low model entropy and thus behave as single-label. S.V. is notably worse than the others once recall drops below about 0.8. All methods make no mistakes at all on WordNet alone, so those results are omitted.

6. **On mixed single-label/multi-label data, M.I. beats semantic entropy (Figures 5cd and 6).** Once high-entropy multi-label WordNet queries are mixed in, S.E.'s performance "degrades noticeably" outside the low-recall region. The authors' diagnosis is a calibration conflict: the predominantly single-label sets and the multi-label set want *different* entropy thresholds, and a single first-order threshold cannot serve both, whereas the MI score is on a scale that does not move with the size of the correct-answer set. The calibrated experiment makes this concrete by partitioning test queries by the entropy of the LLM's output: at low output entropy S.E. and M.I. have similar recall and error, but at high output entropy **S.E.'s recall falls to nearly zero** while M.I. keeps serving answers and "makes only few mistakes". That is the paper's central empirical claim — a much higher recall on high-entropy queries at comparable error rates.

7. **The authors' own reading (Section 7).** They frame epistemic uncertainty as a proxy for truthfulness; claim the contribution is a provable lower bound on epistemic uncertainty; attribute the disentangling ability specifically to considering *joint* distributions over multiple answers rather than marginals; and stress the practical advantage over the prior line of work (Wen et al. 2022, Osband et al. 2023, Johnson et al. 2024) that achieved the same decoupling only by changing the training procedure to use response tuples. Their method needs no retraining, no internal states, and no extra parameters — only iterative prompting. They are candid that the price is prompting the model repeatedly with its own generated responses.

## Open questions the paper itself raises

- **How far can n go, and does it help?** The theory is stated for arbitrary n, the amplification experiments use up to 100 repetitions, but the actual method uses n = 2, justified only as "sufficient... for the benchmarks that we consider". Whether deeper iterative prompting sharpens detection is left open.
- **Whether the ground-truth independence assumption can be relaxed or verified.** The authors flag exactly where it fails (partial answers: a step of an algorithm, part of a story) but offer no test for it and no variant of the bound without it.
- **Long-form outputs.** The paper says F1 matching and log-probabilities become unreliable for longer sequences and filters them out; it notes LLM self-prompting would give better comparisons but is much more expensive. Whether the method survives on long answers is untouched.
- **The looseness of the bound.** Only a lower bound is available; the paper acknowledges that upper-bounding `D_KL(P̃, Q̃)` (the excess risk) would require controlling generalisation, estimation and approximation error, and does not attempt it. Nothing tells the practitioner how much epistemic uncertainty a *low* MI score rules out.
- **Choice of similarity function and threshold.** τ = 0.25 on token-overlap F1 is asserted, not tuned or ablated, and the clustering is assumed to be induced by an equivalence relation, which a thresholded similarity does not in general satisfy.
- **Relation to Ahdritz et al. (2024).** The authors identify this parallel work as closest to theirs, note it may fail on low-uncertainty single-response queries, sketch a two-stage fix with a probability threshold, and observe that tuning that threshold "would introduce extra complications" — an explicitly unexplored comparison.
- **The stabilisation parameters.** Theory prescribes specific `γ1, γ2`; the experiments set both to zero as an approximation for large response sets. The gap between the theoretically valid and the practically used estimator is acknowledged but not quantified.

## Appreciation

**MECHANISM (strong, and cleanly argued).** The core idea is genuinely elegant and is the part worth carrying away. Independence of repeated ground-truth answers is a property that any correct model must satisfy; the prompt family is constructed precisely so that this property is legitimate; the pseudo joint turns a sequential model into a joint distribution over several answers at no training cost; and mutual information is exactly the functional that is blind to marginal spread (aleatoric) and sensitive to inter-coordinate dependence (epistemic). Theorem 4.5's uniformity over all admissible ground truths is what makes the score actionable — you get a certificate about distance from *any* possible truth using only quantities you can sample. The choice of KL direction is well motivated and honestly explained. The estimation analysis is unusually careful for this literature: rather than waving at "we sample 10 responses", the authors confront the infinite-support problem head-on with a missing-mass argument, give both a pessimistic and an effective-support version of the bound, and then go and measure the missing mass on the actual benchmarks. The Section 3 amplification demonstrations are a persuasive, cheap, well-chosen piece of evidence for the underlying intuition, and the attention-head analysis, while idealised, is a real attempt at a why rather than a what.

**MAGNITUDE (modest, and narrower than the framing).** The empirical case is thin relative to the theory. One model (Gemini 1.0 Pro), one domain (short-answer closed-book QA), answers under 20 characters, n = 2, k = 10. On the two natural benchmarks the method **ties** with the existing semantic-entropy baseline; the separation appears only on datasets the authors constructed and then filtered for high entropy in order to "enhance the intended effect". The headline effect — S.E.'s recall collapsing toward zero on high-entropy queries while M.I. keeps answering — is large and is the right diagnostic to look at, but it is measured on 842+842 points (500+500 per calibration split), over 10 repetitions, with Gaussian confidence intervals and no significance testing, on a mixture whose proportions are a design choice. The claimed guarantee also degrades in practice: the experiments use γ1 = γ2 = 0, which is outside the range for which Theorem 4.6 is stated, and Algorithm 3 is a restructured estimator rather than the analysed one. So the size of the practical win is established for a deliberately constructed regime, and the theoretical guarantee and the measured artefact are not quite the same object.

## How it could serve a harness-design effort / limitations / major concerns

**What transfers.** The usable primitive is a cheap, black-box, no-retraining second-order confidence signal: sample a handful of answers, feed them back into a fixed "another answer is X" prompt, and measure whether the model's next answer moves. If it moves a lot, the model is guessing. For a loop that must decide whether to serve, retry, or hand off, this is a gate that — unlike a likelihood or entropy threshold — does not punish a step for having several legitimately valid continuations. That property matters wherever the correct action is genuinely non-unique. The abstention policy is a single thresholded scalar with an explicit calibration recipe, which is easy to slot in beside other gating signals. The Section 3 finding is independently useful as a warning: repeating a claim in the context reliably drags the model toward it, and the amount of drag is itself diagnostic — a model whose answer flips after one contradicting line in context was not confident to begin with.

**Limitations that bite for that use.**
- **Assumption 4.1 excludes exactly the sequential case.** The independence assumption is legitimate only because the fed-back items are complete, interchangeable alternative answers. The authors say directly that it fails if the `Y_i` are partial answers such as a step of an algorithm or a part of a story. Multi-step work is that case. Applying the bound to intermediate steps of a trajectory would break the theorem, not merely loosen it.
- **One-sided by construction.** The method certifies "unreliable" and never certifies "reliable". Low MI is not evidence of correctness; the paper is explicit that it gives a lower bound rather than an upper bound. A stopping rule built on it can say "do not trust this", not "this is done".
- **Cost.** Ten samples plus a quadratic-ish scoring pass over the distinct answers, per decision. Cheap relative to training-based epistemic methods, expensive relative to reading a log-probability, and paid on every judgement.
- **Short-answer scope, with the failure mode named by the authors.** The scoring depends on comparing whole responses, and both the F1 matching and the log-probabilities are stated to be unreliable for long sequences, which is why long responses were filtered out. Long-form or free-text agent output is outside the demonstrated envelope.
- **A calibration set is required per task.** The threshold λ is tuned on hold-out data with a target loss rate, and the paper's own central finding is that thresholds do not transfer across query populations with different aleatoric structure. A harness would need its own calibration data and would need to redo it when the task mix shifts.
- **Single model, single provider.** Everything is Gemini 1.0 Pro. There is no evidence about whether the amplification behaviour, and hence the signal, has the same character across model families or scales — and the related-work section itself notes (Longpre et al. 2021) that larger models are *more* likely to ignore in-context information, which is precisely the variable the method relies on.
- **The estimator used is not the estimator proved.** Algorithm 3 with γ1 = γ2 = 0 differs from Algorithms 1–2 with the prescribed stabilisers. The guarantee should be treated as motivation for the score rather than as a property of the deployed measurement.

**Major concerns.** The main one is the gap between the generality of the framing ("detect when the output of the model is unreliable") and the narrowness of what is shown: a tie with an existing baseline on real benchmarks, and a win on a self-built, entropy-filtered mixture. The second is the assumption's scope — the theorem's independence requirement is exactly what multi-step and long-form generation violate, so the appealing theory does not travel to the loop setting without new work. The third is measurement circularity: F1-at-0.25 is simultaneously the semantic-clustering rule inside the score and the correctness judge in the evaluation.

## Five citations worth chasing

1. **Kuhn, Gal, Farquhar (2023), "Semantic uncertainty" / semantic entropy** — the strongest baseline here, the source of the equivalence-clustering idea, and the method M.I. ties with on natural data and beats only on mixed data. Essential for reading this paper's claims fairly.
2. **Ahdritz, Qin, Vyas, Barak, Edelman (2024), "Distinguishing the knowable from the unknowable with language models" (arXiv:2402.03563)** — named by the authors as the closest parallel and independent work, sharing the observation that high epistemic uncertainty makes an LLM copy from its context, but scoring it by a minimum of conditional entropies instead.
3. **Yadkori, Kuzborskij, Stutz, György, Fisch, Doucet, Beloshapka, Weng, Yang, Szepesvári, Cemgil, Tomasev (2024), "Mitigating LLM hallucinations via conformal abstention" (arXiv:2405.01563)** — the same group's prior work; the source of the calibration procedure used to set the abstention threshold, and the conformal treatment of abstention.
4. **Kadavath et al. (2022), "Language models (mostly) know what they know"** — the self-verification / "ask the model if the answer is True" baseline (S.V. here) and the canonical log-likelihood-thresholding reference; the natural point of comparison for any self-report-based confidence signal.
5. **Johnson, Wen et al. (2024) and Wen et al. (2022), "From predictions to decisions: the importance of joint predictive distributions"** — the prior line that decoupled epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty by *training* on response tuples, discussed at length in Appendix C.3 along with the second-order-calibration condition it needs. This is the alternative route to the same separation, and the reason the present method's training-free character is the claimed advance.
