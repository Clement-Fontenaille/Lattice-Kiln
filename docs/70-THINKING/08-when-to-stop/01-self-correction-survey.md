# When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes? A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs

## Density

**Density: HIGH** — Almost every section carries a load-bearing distinction, a reclassification table, or a checklist item rather than restatement, and a large share of that signal bears directly on the Subject, because the paper's central claim is that a prompted LLM's judgement of its own output is the weak link, and it names the conditions under which that judgement can be trusted.

## Name, keywords, year, venue

**Name.** Ryo Kamoi, Yusen Zhang, Nan Zhang, Jiawei Han, Rui Zhang. "When Can LLMs Actually Correct Their Own Mistakes? A Critical Survey of Self-Correction of LLMs."

**Venue and year.** *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics*, vol. 12, 2024, pp. 1417–1440. Confirmed against the ACL Anthology entry (2024.tacl-1.78), which lists the identical title, author list, volume, year, and page range. TACL is peer-reviewed, and the acknowledgments thank an action editor and anonymous reviewers, which is consistent with the TACL review process. The version read here is arXiv:2406.01297v3, dated 3 December 2024, licensed CC BY 4.0; the v3 revision adds a "Emerging Trends and Recent Developments" section covering work after the original May 2024 cutoff.

**Keywords (mine).** Self-correction; intrinsic self-correction; feedback generation as bottleneck; oracle information; fair versus unfair evaluation; best-possible initial response; verification-easier-than-generation hypothesis; experiment checklist; self-consistency and generate-and-rank as baselines; reclassification of prior results.

## Approach

This is a critical survey rather than a descriptive one, and the difference matters for how it should be read. The authors do not merely group prior self-correction papers into a taxonomy; they argue that a substantial part of the published positive evidence for self-correction does not support the claim it is normally read as supporting, and they re-audit specific papers to show why.

The argument runs in four moves. First, they fix a definition: self-correction is "a framework that refines responses from LLMs using LLMs during inference, possibly with external tools or knowledge." This deliberately spans both the strict sense (a model refining itself with nothing added) and the broad sense (refinement assisted by tools, retrieval, or fine-tuned modules), so that the survey can compare them under one frame. They explicitly place self-consistency and generate-and-rank *outside* the definition, because those methods select among samples rather than refine a response, and they then repurpose those two methods as the baselines that self-correction should have to beat.

Second, they claim prior work conflated three research questions that were being stated as one undifferentiated question, "can LLMs self-correct their mistakes?", and they separate them (Section 3.1, detailed below).

Third, they introduce a second axis that prior taxonomies had ignored. Earlier surveys categorised frameworks by the *source of feedback*; Kamoi et al. argue you must also categorise by the *quality of the initial response*, because whether an experiment can answer a given research question depends on whether the thing being improved was itself generated with best effort. This yields the realistic/unrealistic and fair/unfair categories.

Fourth, they walk through the literature under these new categories, mark specific papers as unrealistic (oracle-using) or unfair (weak initial responses, or asymmetric access to external information), and conclude what survives. The output artifacts are a taxonomy (Figures 2 and 3), a table of representative studies re-labelled by category (Table 1), a table mapping which RQ each paper implicitly targets (Table 2), a table of requirements per RQ (Table 3), a table of unfair settings with the specific defect named per paper (Table 4), and two checklists — one for research claiming self-correction works (Table 7) and one for research reporting negative results (Table 8).

## Models targeted, benchmarks and datasets, scope of the survey

Because this is a survey, the "models" and "benchmarks" are those of the surveyed papers, catalogued rather than run. The authors report no experiments of their own; there is no new benchmark, no new model, and no reproduction. This is worth stating plainly, since it bounds how much the paper's conclusions rest on measurement versus on argument about other people's measurements.

**Models appearing in the surveyed work** (Table 1 and Table 6): GPT-3, GPT-3.5 / GPT-3.5-Turbo, GPT-4 / GPT-4-Turbo, InstructGPT, GPT-Neo (1.3B), GPT-2, PaLM 540B, an unspecified Anthropic 52B model (Constitutional AI revisions), Llama 2 70B, Llama 7B/13B, Minerva 8B and 62B, LLaVA 7B/13B, T5-base and T5-large as feedback models, and PyCodeGPT 110M as a refinement model.

**Tasks and datasets appearing in the surveyed work.** Reasoning: GSM8k, SVAMP, math word problems, text-to-code and code generation, proof generation, logical reasoning, Game of 24, graph colouring, plan generation. Knowledge: closed-book QA, HotpotQA (both closed-book and ground-truth-context variants), Natural Questions, TriviaQA, StrategyQA, 2WikiMultihopQA, ASQA, QReCC, CommonsenseQA. Context-based and open-ended generation: dialogue response generation, topic-based summarisation, constrained generation, sentiment reversal, story generation, detoxification, MT-Bench. Agentic and interactive: MiniWoB++ style computer tasks, game environments (via Reflexion), action planning. Others named in passing: machine translation, information retrieval, vision-language tasks, prompt optimisation.

**Taxonomy — the paper's real scope.** Frameworks are cut three ways descriptively (explicit feedback versus direct refinement; post-hoc versus generation-time / step-level; same-model versus cross-model), and by feedback source (intrinsic prompting; external tools or knowledge; fine-tuning). The consequential cut is the normative one:

- **Unrealistic** — uses information unavailable in deployment, chiefly ground-truth answers. The authors state flatly that these frameworks "cannot be used to verify any research questions."
- **Realistic / Fair** — refines a *best-possible initial response*, defined as an initial response "generated with best effort, using information that self-correction modules can access." Subdivided into **intrinsic** (same model, same information at both stages) and **fair-asymmetric** (self-correction gets extra information, but that information could not easily have improved the initial generation anyway — a code interpreter is their example).
- **Realistic / Unfair (unfair-asymmetric)** — practical, but the initial response was handicapped relative to the correction stage, typically by giving the corrector a resource such as web search that could just as easily have been given to the generator.

**What is out of scope.** Cross-model correction is excluded from RQ1 and RQ2 by construction, since a different model doing the correcting says nothing about a model improving its own output; it remains admissible for RQ3. Training-time self-improvement in general, self-training, self-detection as a standalone task, and editing of human-written text are pushed to a related-work section rather than analysed. The timeframe is papers up to and including May 2024, with post-June-2024 work acknowledged only briefly in Section 12 (reinforcement-learning-based self-correction, and OpenAI's o1). The authors distinguish themselves from Pan et al. (2024), which they characterise as broader but less critical, and from Huang et al. (2024a), which they credit as the motivating analysis but describe as covering only a few intrinsic-self-correction papers in reasoning tasks.

## Author incentive

Four of the five authors are at Penn State University (Kamoi, Yusen Zhang, Nan Zhang, Rui Zhang); Jiawei Han is at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. The corresponding addresses are Kamoi and Rui Zhang at psu.edu. Funding is disclosed as a Cisco Research Grant. There is no product being sold here, no system or benchmark of the authors' own whose adoption the survey would promote, and no proprietary model under evaluation.

The stake that does exist is a position stake, and it is a corrective one. The paper's contribution *is* the negative verdict plus the methodological apparatus (RQ taxonomy, checklists) that produces it, so its value rises with the strength of the claim that prior work over-evaluated self-correction. That is an incentive to read prior papers strictly. Set against it, the strictness is applied through published criteria that a reader can check, and the specific charges are backed by pointers to the surveyed papers' own sections, tables, and in two cases their public prompt files on GitHub, so the audit is falsifiable rather than assertive. One self-citation, Kamoi et al. (2024), is used to support the claim that LLMs often fail to detect their own mistakes; it is cited alongside Tyen et al. (2024) rather than alone.

## Measurement methodology

There is no dependent variable in the usual sense, since nothing is measured. What stands in its place is an adjudication procedure, and it is worth being precise about what that procedure does and does not guarantee.

**Selection of papers.** Coverage is by topic and timeframe rather than by a stated systematic protocol. The authors say they "critically survey broad papers" published up to and including May 2024, and Table 1 is labelled "representative studies" rather than exhaustive. No search strings, no database, no inclusion/exclusion counts, and no inter-rater procedure are reported. This is normal for an NLP survey and is nonetheless a real limit: there is no way for a reader to check whether a fair, positive, intrinsic result exists somewhere in the tail that the survey did not reach. The strong claim — "no prior work demonstrates successful self-correction with feedback from prompted LLMs" — is a claim over a corpus whose boundary is not formally specified, and the authors soften it in the body to "no major work."

**Adjudication of claims.** This is the methodological core, and it is rule-based rather than statistical. Each surveyed paper is asked two questions. (1) Which RQ is it implicitly targeting, judged by what it compares against — comparison only to initial responses indicates RQ1 or RQ2, comparison to competing methods indicates RQ3? (2) Does its framework satisfy the requirements for that RQ, per Table 3 — information symmetry, best-possible initial responses, realism, and the required comparison? A paper failing the requirement for the RQ it targets is reclassified, not refuted: the reported numbers are not disputed, but the interpretation is narrowed to whichever RQ the design can actually support, usually RQ3.

Table 2 makes the conflation visible by showing that single papers target different RQs in different sections. Kim et al. (2023) target RQ1 for arithmetic reasoning (comparing only against their own initial responses) but RQ3 for MiniWoB++ (comparing against baselines). Gou et al. (2024) target RQ2 for arithmetic reasoning and RQ3 for detoxification.

**Evidence quality.** The specific charges are anchored to locations, and in the most contested cases to primary source material. Table 4 names the defect per paper. The appendix reproduces the actual prompts: Table 9 shows that Self-Refine's dialogue-response initial-generation prompt instructs the model to produce responses that are "not interesting and not very engaging," which directly contradicts the task goal that the refinement stage is then scored on; Table 10 shows that Self-Refine's sentiment-reversal few-shot examples for initial generation are drawn from a different transformation than the target one, while the refinement examples all match the target. Both tables cite the URLs of the corresponding files in the Self-Refine repository. That is about as checkable as a survey-level charge gets.

**N and significance.** Not applicable and not treated. No effect sizes are pooled, no meta-analysis is attempted, and no statistical claim is made about the surveyed results. The paper's verdicts are about experimental validity, not about magnitude. A reader looking for "how much does self-correction help, on average" will not find it here, and the authors do not claim to offer it. Notably, they recommend that future work report a measurement they observe is largely missing: the quality of the feedback itself, evaluated directly, for example as error-detection accuracy, rather than only the downstream accuracy of the refined answer.

## Key findings

### The three research questions that prior work conflated

This is the paper's organising contribution, and the distinctions are sharper than the surface wording suggests.

- **RQ1 — Can LLMs self-correct their best-possible initial responses based solely on their inherent capabilities?** This asks about the model alone: no tools, no retrieval, no fine-tuning, same model and same information at both stages. It is answered by showing improvement over the initial response.
- **RQ2 — Can LLMs self-correct their best-possible initial responses assisted by external information?** Tools, retrieval, and fine-tuning are admitted here. It is likewise answered by showing improvement over the initial response, but only if that initial response was itself produced using whatever of that information could have helped it.
- **RQ3 — Are the final outputs from self-correction better than other methods?** This is a question about end performance, not about correction as a mechanism. It does not require a strong starting point, and it cannot be answered by comparing against the initial response; it requires comparison against strong baselines at comparable compute.

Three things make the separation do real work. First, the questions have *different admissible evidence*, laid out in Table 3: RQ1 requires information symmetry, best-possible initial responses, realism, and comparison to initial responses; RQ2 drops the symmetry requirement but keeps the rest; RQ3 drops both symmetry and the best-possible requirement, keeps realism, and swaps comparison-to-initial for comparison-to-strong-baselines. Second, the questions are not nested, so evidence does not flow upward: a demonstration that a pipeline's final output beats alternatives (RQ3) is not evidence that the model corrected itself (RQ1 or RQ2), and this is precisely the inference the authors say readers have been making. Third, a positive RQ3 result is compatible with self-correction being a poor use of the compute it consumes, since self-correction spends multiple model calls and the fair comparison is against baselines allowed the same budget.

The distinction that carries most of the reclassification is the **best-possible initial response**: the initial response must be generated with best effort *using the information the self-correction module itself can access*. This turns a vague worry about weak baselines into a concrete, checkable test — if you could have handed the generator the same resource you handed the critic, and you did not, the experiment measures the resource, not the correction.

### Conditions under which self-correction fails

**Feedback from a prompted LLM, in general tasks, is where it fails.** The headline conclusion is that "no prior work demonstrates successful self-correction with feedback from prompted LLMs," excepting tasks with special structure. In the body this is stated as: no *major* work shows successful self-correction using feedback generated by prompting the model itself, under fair settings, in general tasks. The failure is localised precisely — not in the model's ability to act on a correction, but in its ability to produce one. Section 7 states that prior studies widely agree LLMs *can* refine their responses given reliable feedback; what they cannot reliably do is generate that feedback about their own output without additional information.

This directly qualifies the assumption the whole field was built on. Saunders et al. (2022) had proposed that recognising errors is easier than avoiding them. Kamoi et al.'s verdict is that for current LLMs this holds "only for certain tasks whose verification is exceptionally easy" — the generation-versus-verification asymmetry is a property of the task, not a general property of the model.

Reported negative results are assembled across arithmetic reasoning and closed-book QA (Huang et al., 2024a; Gou et al., 2024), code generation (Gou et al., 2024; Olausson et al., 2024), plan generation (Valmeekam et al., 2023), and graph colouring (Stechly et al., 2023), where intrinsic self-correction does not improve performance and sometimes degrades it. The related-work section adds that even *detection* fails: strong LLMs often cannot detect their own mistakes across various tasks (Tyen et al., 2024; Kamoi et al., 2024).

### Conditions under which self-correction works

Three conditions, and they are all conditions on the feedback rather than on the refinement.

1. **Reliable external feedback is available.** Prior studies "widely agree that self-correction can improve LLM responses when reliable external tools or knowledge suitable for improving feedback are available." The exemplars are code interpreters for code generation, symbolic reasoners for logical reasoning, proof assistants for proof generation, game environments for interactive tasks, and task-specific metrics.
2. **Large-scale fine-tuning.** Fine-tuning feedback and refinement modules does enable self-correction, but Table 6 shows the price: SelFee uses 178K training instances, Volcano 274K, Self-Critique 100K, REFINER 20K–30K. The authors note these studies often substitute a stronger model's feedback for human annotation, which would still require large-scale human annotation to apply at the frontier. The small-data regime is explicitly unexplored.
3. **The task itself is exceptionally suited to correction.** Two structural properties qualify. *Decomposability*: when a response splits into independently checkable parts, verification decomposes into easy sub-tasks — the authors' example is CoVe (Dhuliawala et al., 2024) on list-answer questions such as naming politicians born in New York, where each name can be checked separately. *Easy verifiability*: constrained generation, where you check whether the five required words appear, or Game of 24, where you check whether the arithmetic yields 24. They treat this second class as effectively equivalent to having a strong external tool or oracle for feedback. Their caveat is direct: "many real-world tasks do not satisfy this property." A third, subtler case is admitted under external information — tasks where the response can be used to *obtain* information that was hard to get beforehand, such as generating retrieval queries from a draft answer.

**What "reliable external feedback" means operationally.** The paper does not give a formal definition, and the working meaning has to be read off the cases. It is a signal that (a) comes from outside the model's own judgement — an executor, an interpreter, a proof assistant, a symbolic reasoner, a retrieved document, a metric; (b) is available at inference time in a real deployment, which is what separates it from oracle information; and (c) could not simply have been folded into the initial generation, which is what makes its use *fair* rather than *unfair*. The code interpreter is the clean case on all three counts: it is external, it is deployable, and there is "no easy way" to use it to directly improve the first draft, since you need a draft to execute. Web search fails the third test in RARR-style designs, because the search could have informed the first draft. Ground-truth answers fail the second. Notably, this is a definition by *provenance and availability*, not by accuracy — the paper never quantifies how reliable a signal has to be, nor examines partially reliable feedback, such as a flaky test suite or noisy retrieval, which is arguably the common case in practice.

### Prior positive results that are reclassified, and on what grounds

Two distinct grounds are used, and the paper is careful to keep them apart. Reclassification is not a claim that the numbers are wrong; it is a claim that the numbers answer a narrower question than they were read as answering.

**Ground A — unrealistic, because oracle information enters the loop.** Such results verify no research question at all.

- **RCI Prompting (Kim et al., 2023, §3.1), computer tasks.** Uses ground-truth answers, and does not apply self-correction when the initial response is already correct. Two objections are raised. The design uses information unavailable at deployment, and — this is the sharper point — by skipping correction on already-correct answers it "unfairly ignores mistakes caused by updating correct responses incorrectly," so false-positive corrections never appear in the score.
- **Reflexion (Shinn et al., 2023, §4.2), HotpotQA with ground-truth context.** The feedback signal is an exact match between the generated answer and the ground-truth answer. The critic is therefore not evaluating anything; it is being told the answer. Note that the authors do not condemn Reflexion wholesale — its §4.1 and §4.3 results on games and coding, where feedback comes from game environments and a code interpreter, are classified as fair-asymmetric and stand as RQ2 evidence. The reclassification is section-by-section, not paper-by-paper.

**Ground B — unfair, because the initial response was handicapped.** These results can support RQ3 but not RQ1 or RQ2.

- **Self-Refine (Madaan et al., 2023).** The most concretely documented charge. Initial-response prompts use instructions or few-shot examples that do not correspond to the target task, while the refinement stage gets appropriate ones. For dialogue response generation, the initial prompt asks for responses that are not interesting and not very engaging, then the refinement is credited with making them engaging. For sentiment reversal, the initial-generation few-shot examples demonstrate a different transformation than the target one, while every refinement example matches the target. Both are evidenced with reproduced prompts and links to the authors' own repository.
- **Constitutional AI revisions (Bai et al., 2022), detoxification.** The initial generation is not instructed to avoid harmful output. The authors are explicit that detecting harmful content with LLMs remains a legitimate research topic; their point is narrower — since you can simply instruct the generator to be harmless, this is not correction from a best-possible starting point. The same objection is applied to Wang et al. (2024b).
- **RARR (Gao et al., 2023), and similarly Yu et al. (2023) and Zhao et al. (2023).** Web search is used only in the correction stage while the initial answer is generated closed-book, even though search could have improved the first draft directly. Classified unfair-asymmetric: legitimate as an RQ3 result about final output quality, invalid as RQ2 evidence that self-correction improves a best-possible response.
- **Self-Correction (Welleck et al., 2023, §3.1–3.3).** Stronger fine-tuning is applied to the correction model than to the initial generator, so again the starting point is not the best available under the same resources. RQ3-admissible, RQ2-inadmissible.

The residue after this audit is what makes the survey's headline claim: what survives as fair RQ2 evidence is dominated by external-tool cases (Self-Debug, CRITIC with an interpreter, Reflexion on games and coding, Baldur with a proof assistant) and by large-data fine-tuning cases, while the fair intrinsic-prompting cell is essentially empty outside decomposable or trivially verifiable tasks.

### The checklist for a fair self-correction experiment

Table 7 gives requirements per target RQ. Required of all three: state the target RQ and the framework category explicitly; use no oracle information such as ground-truth answers; when fine-tuning, report the number of annotations and the computational cost needed to reach the reported performance. Recommended of all three: evaluate feedback quality directly, for instance error-detection accuracy, rather than only downstream performance. Required of RQ1 and RQ2: use sufficiently strong prompts for the initial response. Required of RQ1 alone: use intrinsic self-correction. Required when external tools or knowledge are used: apply them to improve the initial generation as far as possible. Required when fine-tuning for correction: fine-tune the initial generator as well, as far as possible. Recommended: report the minimum training-data size at which self-correction emerges, and evaluate cross-model correction of a *stronger* model's mistakes. Required of RQ3: compare against strong baselines at comparable computational cost.

Table 8 is the mirror checklist for *negative* results, and it is a genuinely even-handed move — the authors hold sceptics to a standard too. Required: state which RQ the result refutes and for which framework category; use strong prompts for the self-correction stage, for instance state-of-the-art reference-free metrics, so that a weak critic prompt is not mistaken for a limit of the model; and when no realistically available external tools or knowledge are used, say explicitly that the evaluation was run under weak conditions. Recommended: evaluate with tools and knowledge that would actually be available in deployment.

### Baselines that self-correction should have to beat

Because self-correction spends several model calls, RQ3 must be settled against methods allowed similar compute. The paper nominates two families it deliberately excludes from the definition of self-correction. **Self-consistency** samples several responses and takes a majority vote, extended beyond reasoning to text and code generation. **Generate-and-rank** samples several responses and selects with a verifier — post-hoc ranking by self-evaluation, by confidence, by fine-tuned verifiers, or by tool-backed verifiers; or feedback-guided decoding that selects per reasoning step using generation probability, prompted self-evaluation, or fine-tuned verifiers. Tree-of-Thought is placed here rather than under self-correction. The authors' verdict on RQ3 is a null one: self-correction is often not compared against sufficiently strong baselines, so whether it beats them remains unclear. Only a few studies make the comparison at all, against self-consistency (Gou et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024a) or pass@k for code (Zhang et al., 2023a; Olausson et al., 2024).

### Bearing on the stop-or-continue decision in a loop

The survey is framed around single-pass answer refinement, and it does not study stopping criteria, budget exhaustion, or hand-off as topics in their own right. Nothing in it measures when a loop should terminate. Several findings nonetheless bear on that decision, and one bears on it very directly.

The direct one is the RCI entry in Table 4. The oracle there is annotated specifically as a **stop condition**: ground-truth answers determine when correction is applied and when it is skipped, and correct responses are left untouched. The authors' objection is exactly the failure mode a real loop faces — with a genuine stop rule you cannot know which attempts are already correct, so you must expose correct answers to revision, and the false-positive corrections that follow are part of the true cost. Their charge is that this cost was excluded from the measurement by fiat. Read as a claim about loops, it says that a reported iterative-correction gain obtained under an oracle stop condition tells you nothing about the same loop run with a self-assessed stop condition, since the whole difficulty has been assumed away.

The rest is indirect but load-bearing. The central finding is about *feedback generation*, and a stop-or-continue decision is a feedback judgement — "is this attempt correct or done" is the same self-evaluation the paper concludes prompted LLMs cannot perform reliably on their own output. The self-detection literature they summarise strengthens this: models often fail to detect their own mistakes at all, which is a weaker requirement than correcting them. Their positive conditions transfer with the same shape — a stop signal from an executor, a test suite, a proof assistant, or a symbolic checker inherits the reliability of that tool, whereas a stop signal that is the model's own prompted verdict does not. The decomposability and easy-verifiability exemptions transfer likewise: a loop over a task whose result is cheaply checkable can be stopped on that check. The paper also gestures at the machinery a stop rule would need, listing confidence-based approaches under future directions — generation probabilities (Varshney et al., 2023; Jiang et al., 2023b), prompted confidence (Li et al., 2024a), and logical-consistency checks that generate new questions from an answer (Jung et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2024) — but presents these as unexplored directions for improving feedback, not as validated stopping criteria.

Two further transfers are worth naming because they cut against loops rather than for them. The false-positive concern generalises: a system that revises without a reliable check can turn correct work incorrect, and iteration multiplies the exposure. And the RQ3 argument applies with more force to loops than to single passes, since a loop's compute cost grows with each iteration while the baselines it should be compared against — sampling several attempts and voting or verifying among them — spend the same budget without needing the model to judge itself.

## Open questions the paper itself raises

Section 11 states four, and the analysis leaves several more implicit.

**Stated.** *Improving feedback.* Most intrinsic-self-correction studies used simple prompts for feedback, so the negative verdict may be partly a verdict on prompt quality rather than on capability; the authors suggest applying reference-free, point-wise LLM-based evaluation metrics, human-written evaluation criteria, and decomposition-based evaluation, and they list confidence-based approaches as a second direction. *Unexplored tasks.* Because verification difficulty varies by task and the field concentrated on maths and code, there may be tasks where intrinsic correction works that nobody tried; they point at open-ended generation, where LLM-based evaluation is known to function reasonably, as the likely place to look. *Fine-tuning on small training data.* Whether self-correction can be trained without six-figure annotation sets is open; reinforcement learning and self-corrective learning avoid human feedback but need a usable reward function, which most tasks lack — they name RL4F's use of ROUGE for summarisation and action planning as sub-optimal. *Pre-training.* That large-scale fine-tuning is needed at all suggests current pre-training does not instil self-correction, and pre-training strategies that would are unexplored.

**Also left open by the analysis.** RQ3 is simply unresolved: whether self-correction beats self-consistency or generate-and-rank at equal compute is not known, because the comparisons were rarely run. Cross-model correction of a *stronger* model's output is flagged as under-evaluated. The minimum data threshold at which fine-tuned correction emerges is unmeasured. And the reliability of feedback is never made a graded quantity, so how good an external signal must be before it helps is not addressed.

## Appreciation

**Mechanism.** The paper's durable contribution is a set of distinctions that make a previously slippery claim checkable, and they hold up under scrutiny. Separating RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3 identifies a real and consequential inference error, since demonstrating a good final output is not demonstrating a working correction mechanism. The best-possible-initial-response criterion converts a vague objection into a specific and falsifiable test, and it is applied symmetrically: the same criterion that invalidates a positive result as RQ2 evidence explicitly preserves it as RQ3 evidence. The realistic/unrealistic and fair/unfair axes correctly identify that a taxonomy organised only by feedback source cannot tell a valid experiment from an invalid one. Localising the bottleneck in feedback generation rather than in refinement is the single most useful mechanistic claim, because it tells you which component to fix or replace. The recommendation to measure feedback quality directly is the natural consequence and is, on the evidence assembled here, plainly correct. The negative-results checklist is a mark of seriousness, since it would have been easy to publish the sceptical half alone.

**Magnitude.** This should be kept firmly separate, because the paper supplies almost no magnitudes. It reports no experiments, pools no effect sizes, and offers no statistics; every quantitative claim in it is a restatement of a surveyed paper's own numbers or a count of training instances. So "self-correction works with reliable external feedback" is a claim about direction and condition, not about size — the paper gives no basis for saying whether tool-backed correction buys a large or a marginal improvement in any given setting, nor how the gain scales with iterations, model strength, or task difficulty. Equally, "no prior work shows successful intrinsic self-correction" is an absence-of-evidence claim over an informally bounded corpus, not a measured effect of zero. The strongest empirical objects in the paper are the annotation counts in Table 6, which are concrete and striking, and the reproduced Self-Refine prompts, which are documentary evidence rather than measurement. Read for mechanism, the paper is strong; read for magnitude, it is close to silent, and the authors do not pretend otherwise.

## How it could serve a harness-design effort, limitations, major concerns

**What it offers a harness.** Read as engineering guidance, the paper reduces to a placement rule: put the judgement where a reliable signal exists, and do not ask a prompted model to grade its own work in tasks where verification is not structurally easier than generation. That rule has direct consequences for a loop's design. It argues for routing the done-or-not decision to executors, test suites, type checkers, proof assistants, and other deterministic checks wherever the task admits them, and for treating a model's own verdict as a weak signal elsewhere. It supplies a triage question for any new task — is the response decomposable, or cheaply verifiable? — that predicts whether self-assessment will work at all. It warns that correction without a reliable check can damage correct work, which for a loop means the revision policy needs a floor and not only a ceiling. It supplies the honest comparison for any iterative refinement design, namely sampling several attempts and selecting among them at the same budget. And it supplies an instrumentation recommendation that transfers cleanly: measure the accuracy of the judge, not only the quality of the final artifact, because the two can diverge and the paper's whole case is that the field learned little by measuring only the latter. The evaluation checklists are also directly usable as a design review for any internal experiment claiming that a self-assessment mechanism works.

**Limitations, taken at their strongest.** The corpus boundary is informal — no search protocol, no counts, no exhaustiveness claim — so the universal negative rests on coverage a reader cannot verify, and the authors' own hedge from "no prior work" to "no major work" acknowledges this. The negative verdict is also confounded with prompt quality by the authors' own admission: they note that most intrinsic studies used simple feedback prompts and list better prompting as an open direction, which means "prompted LLMs cannot generate reliable feedback" is partly "nobody has yet tried hard enough," and their own Table 8 requires sceptics to rule this out. Reliability of external feedback is treated as binary when in practice it is graded, and the intermediate regime — partial test coverage, noisy retrieval, a linter that catches some faults — is precisely the regime most real systems occupy and is not analysed. The paper is a document review rather than a reproduction, so classifications rest on what surveyed papers reported about themselves, though the appendix prompt evidence shows the authors did go to primary sources where it mattered most. The May 2024 cutoff is a hard limit that the brief Section 12 does not repair: reinforcement-learning-based self-correction and o1-style models are named as a trend and then not analysed, and since a model trained to recognise and revise its own errors is exactly the case that could move the RQ1 verdict, the paper's central negative claim is scoped to a model generation that its own final section says was ending.

**Major concerns for transfer.** Two stand out. First, there is a scope mismatch with the Subject: this paper is about improving an answer, whereas a loop must decide whether to continue, stop, or hand off, and those are different decisions with different cost structures — a wrong stop wastes budget or ships bad work, while a wrong revision corrupts good work. The paper's findings constrain the loop case by analogy, through the shared dependence on self-evaluation, but no result here was measured on a stopping decision. That analogy is strong on mechanism and untested on magnitude, and it should be carried across as a hypothesis rather than as a finding. Second, the framework's normative categories were built to adjudicate *research claims*, and they do not translate one-to-one into deployment advice. "Unfair" means that an experiment cannot support a particular RQ; it does not mean the pipeline is bad engineering. A system that retrieves only when checking a draft may be entirely sensible in production, and the paper would classify it as unfair. Anyone using this vocabulary to evaluate a design rather than a paper needs to keep that distinction, which the paper itself observes when it repeatedly notes that unfair frameworks remain valid for RQ3.

## Five citations worth chasing next

1. **Huang et al. (2024a), "Large Language Models Cannot Self-Correct Reasoning Yet."** The paper the authors name as motivating their own, and the principal source of the negative results in reasoning tasks. It is also one of the few studies to compare self-correction against self-consistency, which makes it the closest thing here to RQ3 evidence.
2. **Kamoi et al. (2024) and Tyen et al. (2024), on self-detection of mistakes.** Cited jointly for the finding that strong LLMs often cannot detect their own errors at all. Detection is a weaker requirement than correction and is the closer analogue of a stop-or-continue judgement, so this is the most directly Subject-relevant thread leaving the paper.
3. **Shinn et al. (2023), "Reflexion."** Worth reading precisely because the survey splits it: §4.2 is dismissed as oracle-driven, while §4.1 and §4.3 stand as fair evidence with game environments and a code interpreter. It is also the canonical iterative-agent-loop paper in this literature, so it is where the loop framing and the refinement framing actually meet.
4. **Welleck et al. (2023), "Self-Correction" / self-corrective learning.** The training-time route that avoids human feedback by selecting model-generated feedback that demonstrably improved a response — a mechanism for bootstrapping a verifier from outcomes, which is the shape a learned stop rule would take. The survey flags its evaluation as unfairly fine-tuned, so it needs to be read critically.
5. **The confidence-and-consistency cluster listed under future directions** — Varshney et al. (2023) and Jiang et al. (2023b) on generation-probability confidence, Li et al. (2024a) on prompted confidence, and Jung et al. (2022) with Wu et al. (2024) on generating questions from an answer to test logical consistency. These are the candidate signals for a continue-or-stop decision that does not depend on the model grading itself directly, and the survey identifies them as promising and unvalidated.

A sixth, if compute comparisons matter: **Wang et al. (2023) on self-consistency**, together with **Cobbe et al. (2021) and Lightman et al. (2024) on trained verifiers**, as the baseline family that any iterative design has to beat at an equal budget.
