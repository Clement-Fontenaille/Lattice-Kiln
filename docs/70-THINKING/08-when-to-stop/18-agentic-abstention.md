# Agentic Abstention: Do Agents Know When to Stop Instead of Act?

## Density

**Density: HIGH.** Most of the paper is load-bearing, and the load-bearing part sits almost exactly on the Subject: the paper defines a metric that separates *whether* an agent stops from *when* it stops, and it reports that the timing half is where every system it tests fails. The padding is confined to the appendices, where roughly forty pages of prompts, rewriting templates and AbstentionBench dataset descriptions restate things the main text already establishes.

## Name, keywords, year, venue

**Keywords (mine).** Agentic abstention; timely stopping; abstention recall at K; latent infeasibility; environment-revealed infeasibility; over-abstention; oracle warranted step; context engineering; stopping-rule playbook; POMDP formulation of stopping; scaffold effects.

**Venue.** None. This is an arXiv preprint (cs.AI), version 1, with no venue or peer-review status attached. The paper is written in a conference format and includes a Limitations and a Broader Impacts appendix, which suggests it was prepared for a machine-learning conference submission.

**Authors.** Han Luo (University of Leeds, Southwest Jiaotong University, and University of Washington), Bingbing Wen (University of Washington), and Lucy Lu Wang (University of Washington and the Allen Institute for AI). Luo and Wen are marked as equal contributors.

## Approach

The authors argue that the abstention literature has studied a single-turn decision, in which a model either answers a question or declines to answer it, and that this framing does not describe what an agent faces. An agent that acts in an environment has a third option at every turn, which is to keep gathering information. The authors therefore define **Agentic Abstention** as the problem of deciding *when* an agent should stop acting under uncertainty, and they treat it as a sequential decision problem rather than a one-shot classification.

They formalise the setting as a partially observable Markov decision process with the action space `{ANSWER, ABSTAIN, ACT}`. `ANSWER` covers any terminal completion action, such as returning a final answer in question answering, purchasing an item in a shopping environment, or submitting a solution in a terminal environment. `ABSTAIN` covers a terminal refusal to proceed under the current information state. `ACT` covers any non-terminal environment interaction, such as issuing a search query or clicking a button. The episode ends when the agent chooses `ANSWER` or `ABSTAIN`, or when the step budget of ten turns is exhausted. The latent state includes whether the task is resolvable at all, and that property is precisely what the agent cannot observe directly. The authors keep the reward function out of the formulation deliberately, because they are not optimising a policy; they use the POMDP only to express partial observability and history-dependence.

One definitional choice matters for the Subject. The authors fold **asking the user for clarification into `ABSTAIN`** rather than treating it as a fourth action. Their argument is that a clarification request is also a decision to stop short of answering under the current information state. In their evaluation a clarification request counts as an abstention, and the episode terminates the moment it is issued. In the Related Work appendix they restate this as viewing clarification as a natural follow-up *after* abstaining, not as an alternative to it.

The second core move is the distinction between two ways a task can be unsolvable.

- **Request-based Abstention.** The instruction itself is defective, so a careful agent should stop at turn one without touching the environment. The sub-categories are False Premise or Contradiction, Subjective Preference, and Underspecified Intent.
- **Environment-based Abstention.** The instruction is well formed and looks solvable, and the environment has been modified so that it cannot be satisfied. Infeasibility becomes visible only after the agent interacts. In the web setting this is called **Missing Target**, and in the terminal setting **Missing Prerequisite**.

This second category is the paper's real experimental object, and it is the closest thing in the paper to the Subject's "has stalled, or should be abandoned" signal arriving from the trajectory rather than from the prompt.

Finally, the authors propose **CONVOLVE** (Context Evolution), a context-engineering method that reads full interaction trajectories and distils them into reusable stopping rules, which are then appended to the agent's system prompt on later episodes. No model parameters are updated.

## Models targeted

The paper evaluates thirteen LLM-as-agent systems and two agent scaffolds. The models are GPT-5.4-mini, Grok 4.1 Fast, Llama-3.3-8B and 70B Instruct, GPT-OSS-120B, MiniMax-M2.5, Qwen-3 at 8B, 14B and 32B, Qwen-3-235B-A22B in both Instruct and Thinking variants, Gemma-4-31B-it, and GLM-5.1. The two scaffolds are Terminus 2, which the authors treat as a deliberately simple neutral testbed, and Codex CLI. Proprietary models are accessed through first-party APIs, and open-weight models are served through Together AI and OpenRouter.

Coverage is scenario-specific rather than a full model-by-scaffold grid, which the authors justify on cost grounds. WebShop carries the broad cross-model comparison with eight systems. Question answering uses five models. The terminal setting fixes the base model to GPT-5.4-mini and varies the scaffold and the reasoning effort, so every terminal number in the paper describes one base model. Scaling analysis uses the Qwen-3 family at four sizes. The reasoning analysis compares Qwen-3-235B Instruct against Thinking in the web setting, and compares GPT-5.4-mini at low, medium and high reasoning effort in the terminal setting. CONVOLVE is run only on Llama-3.3-8B and 70B.

## Benchmarks and datasets

The consolidated benchmark contains more than 28,000 instructions, assembled from three sources.

**WebShop**, adapted from Yao et al. (2022a). The authors take the first 500 test instructions, following the WebShop and AgentBench implementations, and keep those as solvable controls. They then build 500 abstain-warranted instances against the same instruction pool. For the 249 request-based instances they rewrite the instruction with GPT-5.4-mini at high reasoning effort, targeting the three request-based categories, and the authors then manually review, revise or filter every rewrite. For the 251 Missing Target instances they leave the instruction untouched, delete the ground-truth target item from the product catalogue, and rebuild the Lucene search index so that the deleted item cannot be retrieved at all. The final set is 1,000 instances at a 1:1 solvable-to-unsolvable ratio. The authors check that the rewrites are not trivially detectable, reporting overlapping token-length distributions and a t-SNE plot of instruction embeddings in which original and rewritten instructions are, in their words, semantically indistinguishable.

**Terminal-Bench 2.0**, adapted from Merrill et al. (2026), which supplies 89 hard Dockerised long-horizon tasks, each with an instruction, an initialised container, verification tests and a human reference solution. The request-based rewrites go through a dual-agent pipeline: a rewriting agent proposes the variant, and a validation agent audits it against explicit category rules, with up to three revision rounds. The validation prompt is strict in a useful way, because it fails a rewrite that merely wraps the original in a generic prefix, that uses more than one mechanism, that contains meta-language, or that accidentally converts an instruction defect into an environment failure. The pipeline produced 87 False Premise or Contradiction and 84 Underspecified Intent variants, and author review retained 87 and 80 respectively. For the environment-based variants the authors hand-selected 21 tasks whose reference solutions depended on an identifiable resource, then removed or disabled that resource, which may be a file, a dataset, a package, a configuration entry, a permission, a service or an external capability. The final set is 277 instances, of which 89 are solvable and 188 are not.

**AbstentionBench**, adapted from Kirichenko et al. (2025). The authors retain 16 of its constituent datasets and 27,073 samples, and they deliberately drop FreshQA, MuSiQue, SQuAD 2.0, QASPER and the Temporal subset of CoCoNot, on the grounds that search can legitimately resolve many of those questions. They then convert each static prompt into a sequential episode in which the agent chooses among ANSWER, ABSTAIN and SEARCH at every turn, with at most ten search calls. Retrieval runs against a frozen `enwiki-20260101` Wikipedia dump, chunked to 100 words with FlashRAG and encoded with E5-base, returning the top three passages per call. The five should-abstain categories are Answer Unknown, False Premise, Subjective, Underspecified Context, and Underspecified Intent.

A small methodological aside in Appendix H is worth recording. The authors also tried FalseQA and then excluded it, because they found that the agent's non-abstention there was usually *correct* behaviour rather than a failure: the model recognises the false premise, corrects it, and answers the corrected question, which the authors judge a reasonable response. Two GPT-5.4-mini transcripts are shown to support that judgement.

## Measurement methodology

All metrics are defined at the episode level rather than the step level, and Appendix F gives them formally. The two quantities that carry the paper are the agent's first abstention step and an annotated oracle step.

For episode `i`, `τ_i` is the first step at which the agent emits the abstain action, and it is set to infinity if the agent never abstains. For every abstain-warranted episode, the authors annotate `w_i`, the **oracle warranted step**, defined as the earliest step from which abstention is justified. An abstention counts as correct only when `w_i ≤ τ_i < ∞`. Abstaining *earlier* than `w_i` is scored as a failure, and the authors say explicitly why: without that rule an agent would collect credit for refusing before it had seen the evidence that makes refusal correct. This is the design decision that makes the benchmark measurable, because it prevents a reflexively cautious agent from topping the leaderboard.

The dependent variables are then:

- **AbsRec@K**, the fraction of abstain-warranted episodes on which the agent abstains correctly within K steps. It is monotone non-decreasing in K by construction.
- **Timely Recall**, which the authors define as AbsRec@1 in their notation, meaning `τ_i = w_i` exactly. Note that AbsRec@1 here counts abstention at the earliest *warranted* step, not at the first turn of the episode. For request-based tasks `w_i = 1` and timely recall does reduce to first-turn recall. For environment-based tasks `w_i = 2`, because the agent must interact once before infeasibility is visible, so the correct timely step is turn two.
- **Overall Recall**, which is AbsRec@10 under the fixed budget of `K_max = 10`.
- **SPL**, adapted from the embodied-navigation literature (Anderson et al., 2018), computed as the mean over positive episodes of `S_i · w_i / max(τ_i, w_i)`. It rewards correct abstention and discounts it by how late the abstention arrived. Missed and premature abstentions contribute zero.
- **Over-abstention rate**, the fraction of *solvable* control instances on which the agent wrongly abstains. This is reported cumulatively by turn.

The Subject asks whether the benchmark has should-not-abstain controls, and it does. WebShop is balanced 1:1 with 500 solvable instances. Terminal-Bench retains all 89 original solvable tasks. AbstentionBench carries answerable questions natively. So over-abstention is measurable in principle, although the paper reports the over-abstention curves only for the web and terminal settings and not for QA.

**How abstention is detected.** There is no LLM judge in the loop, and this is a strength worth stating plainly. Abstention is a structurally emitted action, so detection is a rule rather than a judgement. In the QA setting the agent must return JSON with `{"action": "ABSTAIN", "reason": ...}` chosen from an explicit three-action space, and the prompt tells the model outright that it should abstain rather than search forever once evidence stays insufficient. In WebShop the agent calls a dedicated `abstain_action` tool alongside the normal search and click tools, and the system prompt names the conditions under which it should be called. In Terminal-Bench the agent returns its normal JSON with `"commands": []`, `"task_complete": true`, `"abstain": true`, plus an abstain category and reason. The consequence is that parsing the abstention event needs no validation, because the action is explicit.

The oracle step `w_i`, by contrast, is *not* derived from the trajectory. It is fixed by construction as 1 for request-based tasks and 2 for environment-based tasks, and no per-instance human annotation of the true earliest-recognisable step is reported. The `w_i = 2` value assumes that a single interaction suffices to reveal the missing target or the missing prerequisite. The paper does not test that assumption, and it does not report inter-annotator agreement on it.

**What is controlled.** The scaffold-effect comparison is the cleanest experiment in the paper, because Codex CLI and Terminus 2 are run against the same GPT-5.4-mini base model. The CONVOLVE evaluation is also tightly controlled, in that the baseline is the same 70B model on the same 101 held-out examples with the provider, temperature, token limit, interaction limit, timeout and retry policy all held fixed, and the only difference is whether the learned playbook is injected. The CONVOLVE data splits are stratified by abstention scenario and grouped by the originating WebShop goal or Terminal-Bench task, so variants derived from one underlying task cannot straddle the train and test split. That grouping matters here, because the abstention variants are systematic rewrites of a small pool of source tasks.

**N and statistical treatment.** The headline N of 28,000-plus is dominated by AbstentionBench, at 27,073 samples. The agentic portions are far smaller, at 1,000 WebShop instances and 277 Terminal-Bench instances, of which only 251 and 21 respectively are the environment-based cases the paper argues are the hardest and most novel. The CONVOLVE experiments are smaller again: 20 training trajectories, 8 validation examples, one epoch, at most two reflection and update rounds per example, and a 101-example held-out evaluation set.

Significance is not treated at all. The paper reports no confidence intervals, no error bars, no variance across seeds, and no significance tests anywhere. Every number is a single point estimate from what appears to be one run per configuration. On the 21 environment-based terminal tasks, and on 101-example held-out sets, that omission is material: a swing of a few instances moves the reported percentages by several points. The authors do not discuss run-to-run variance, and they do not state a temperature for the main evaluation, although they do say temperature is held fixed between the CONVOLVE arms.

## Key findings

**1. The failure is timing, not capability.** This is the paper's central claim, and the metric design is what lets them separate the two. Abstention recall rises with the interaction budget, which means many agents do eventually work out that a task is impossible. Timely recall stays low everywhere. Every system tested achieves under 40% average timely recall, and most sit in the 0.0 to 0.3 range for AbsRec@1. The authors' own reading is that agents "usually abstain too late or not at all, expending many wasteful actions." Two exceptions are named: Llama-3.3-70B in the web setting, and Qwen3-235B in QA.

**2. Absolute levels are low even with the full budget.** Six of eight web models stay below 0.5 AbsRec@10. Both terminal configurations stay below 0.5 AbsRec@10. Four of five QA models stay below 0.5 AbsRec@10. In the web setting the strongest baseline, Llama-3.3-70B, reaches 26.7% timely recall against 83.2% overall recall, which is roughly a threefold gap between eventually stopping and stopping on time. The best terminal configuration reaches only 21.6% timely recall.

**3. Environment-revealed infeasibility is the hardest case, and it is the one that matters for a loop.** Category breakdowns show Missing Target as the hardest web category, precisely because the instruction reads as solvable and only interaction exposes the problem. False Premise is the *easiest* web category, since the contradiction is legible in the instruction text. In the terminal setting Underspecified Intent is hardest for both scaffolds. In QA, performance is best on Answer Unknown, and False Premise is consistently difficult. The authors' summary is that the categories requiring environmental evidence or missing-intent recognition produce the largest gap between timely and eventual abstention.

**4. Scale improves eventual abstention but not timely abstention.** Across Qwen-3 at 8B, 14B, 32B and 235B, larger models reach higher overall recall while timely recall barely moves. The authors read this as scale helping a model abstain *eventually* but not *earlier*. The abstract states the stronger version of this, that larger or more capable models sometimes perform worse at timely abstention, and the paper also notes that stronger base models do not always abstain better.

**5. Reasoning trades timely recall against overall recall.** Qwen-3-235B-Thinking improves timely recall over the Instruct variant but *lowers* overall recall. The authors' interpretation is that reasoning helps the model catch the earliest stopping point, and helps very little once that moment has passed. In the terminal setting, medium reasoning effort gives GPT-5.4-mini the best trade-off, and moving from medium to high yields no further gain. This is a non-monotonicity in reasoning effort, not a straight line.

**6. Over-abstention grows with interaction length, and is environment-dependent.** On solvable web tasks the cumulative over-abstention rate for Qwen3-235B-Instruct climbs to 34% by turn 10, and to 24% for the Thinking variant. Terminal tasks show a far weaker effect: GPT-5.4-mini at low reasoning plateaus near 8%, while medium and high reasoning stay at 0 to 2%. The authors attribute the web effect to accumulated uncertainty from additional search, and they note that stronger reasoning reduces false abstention without eliminating the benchmark-dependent gap. The direct implication is that the *same* longer trajectory that eventually produces a correct stop on impossible tasks also produces spurious stops on possible ones.

**7. The scaffold matters as much as the model.** With GPT-5.4-mini held fixed, Codex CLI reaches about 0.38 AbsRec@10 while Terminus 2 reaches about 0.18, and Codex CLI leads at every value of K and across every category. The authors conclude that abstention is shaped not only by the base model but by how the scaffold structures interaction with the environment. The paper does not decompose *which* scaffold property produces the difference.

**8. CONVOLVE substantially improves timely stopping, and its lessons transfer downward and upward.** On the WebShop held-out set, Llama-3.3-70B with its own learned playbook rises from 26.7 to 57.4 timely recall, from 83.2 to 100.0 overall recall, and from 55.3 to 78.9 SPL. Strikingly, a playbook learned by the *8B* model lifts the 70B model almost as far, to 55.3 timely recall, 99.0 overall recall and 76.4 SPL, so the useful content of the playbook is not tied to the capability of the model that wrote it. The gains for the 8B model as a consumer are much smaller, rising only from 6.9 to 7.9 with its own lessons and to 12.9 with the 70B's lessons, which suggests the 8B model can articulate usable stopping rules better than it can follow them. A plain in-context-learning baseline is also reported, and it is competitive on the 70B model, at 55.1 timely recall, while it actually *degrades* the 8B model's overall recall from 92.1 to 81.1. Appendix G repeats the experiment on AbstentionBench and Terminal-Bench, and the terminal gains are the largest in the paper, with Llama-3.3-70B rising from 37.6 to 68.9 timely recall and from 66.2 to 79.1 overall recall.

The playbook content shown in Appendix J is worth noting because it is unremarkable in the best way. The learned rules are short imperatives, such as abstaining when the observation already shows the request cannot be satisfied, avoiding repeated search or click loops once the environment has shown a request is unfulfillable, and treating invalid actions as an early signal rather than something to retry through. Nothing in the playbook is environment-specific in a way that would fail to transfer, and one entry is essentially a self-scored fulfillability check.

One caveat the paper itself flags on CONVOLVE: the reported gains are on the abstention-only subset, so the tables show abstention improving. The authors state that task completion is preserved on solvable tasks, and describe the goal as preserving completion ability, but the main CONVOLVE table reports only AbsRec@1, AbsRec@10 and SPL. No over-abstention rate or task-success number for the playbook-augmented agents appears in either Table 1 or Table 3.

### The named failure modes

The paper names four distinct ways an agent mishandles the stopping decision, and they are worth separating because they call for different fixes.

1. **Never abstaining.** The agent burns the whole budget on an impossible task. This is the dominant failure at low AbsRec@10.
2. **Abstaining late.** The agent does stop, but only after many unnecessary tool calls. This is the paper's main quantitative target, and it is what SPL penalises.
3. **Looping.** The playbook entries and the reflection signals name repeated search or click loops explicitly, along with delayed responses to invalid actions, so a repeated-action signature is treated as evidence that the agent has missed a stopping point.
4. **Substituting an ungrounded default for missing grounding.** This is the analogue of the hallucinated-context failure that the Subject asks about, and Appendix H documents it in a terminal transcript. GPT-5.4-mini is asked to build a probability distribution whose vocabulary size must match a target LLM's tokenizer. It searches the environment for tokenizer metadata, finds none, discovers that `transformers`, `tokenizers`, `torch`, `tiktoken` and `sentencepiece` are all unavailable, states in its own reasoning that it will have to infer a reasonable target vocabulary size, then instantiates `n = 100256` and proceeds to solve the task against that invented number. The agent's own trace shows it identified the missing grounding and then chose fabrication over stopping. The parallel QA failure is shown on ALCUNA, where the agent searches for a fictional species, receives three irrelevant passages about real bison, and answers "herd" at turn two.

## Open questions the paper itself raises

Drawn from Appendix A (Limitations) and Appendix B (Broader Impacts):

- The three environments are acknowledged as representative slices only. The authors name richer interfaces, private tools, long-lived user state and multi-step workflows as uncovered.
- The environment-based tasks cover only missing targets and missing prerequisites. The authors list the latent-infeasibility forms they did *not* build: permission boundaries, stale external resources, conflicting tool outputs, and dependence on prior user context. Conflicting tool outputs seems the most consequential omission, because it is the case where the trajectory contains contradictory rather than absent evidence.
- Findings are a snapshot of current systems, not properties of the underlying models, precisely because abstention depends jointly on the model and the scaffold.
- Contamination cannot be ruled out, since the benchmark derives from public datasets. The authors propose closed, dynamically generated or continuously updated evaluation environments as the fix.
- Abstention should not be optimised as a standalone objective. The authors state directly that agents abstaining too often become less useful, frustrate users, and shift responsibility back to the user in cases where the agent could reasonably have helped, so abstention must be evaluated jointly with task success and over-abstention.
- They flag that in deployment, abstention will need to combine with clarification, escalation and human oversight, and they call their own benchmark unsuitable as a deployment policy.

## Appreciation

**Mechanism.** The mechanism contributions are the durable part of this paper, and I would rate them highly independently of any number in it.

The first is the decomposition of the stopping decision into *whether* and *when*, operationalised as the pair (AbsRec@1, AbsRec@10) against an oracle warranted step. A single abstention-accuracy figure conflates an agent that stops immediately with one that stops on turn nine, and those are different systems with very different costs. The gap between the two numbers is itself the useful diagnostic, and Llama-3.3-70B's 26.7 against 83.2 is a more informative description of that agent than either number alone.

The second is scoring premature abstention as failure. Without that rule, the metric rewards a policy of refusing everything, and the entire benchmark becomes gameable. It is a small formal choice that makes the rest coherent.

The third is the request-based versus environment-based split. This separates a text-comprehension task, in which the instruction is visibly defective, from a genuinely sequential task, in which the agent must integrate an observation against its plan and conclude that the plan is dead. These are different competences, and the paper's own category results confirm they dissociate: False Premise is the easiest web category while Missing Target is the hardest.

The fourth is that abstention is an explicit typed action rather than a judged property of free text. This removes the LLM-judge validation problem from the measurement entirely.

The fifth is CONVOLVE's demonstration that stopping competence can be moved into the context rather than the weights, and that the artefact which carries it is a short natural-language rule list. The 8B-to-70B transfer result is the interesting one, because it separates *generating* a stopping rule from *executing* one.

**Magnitude.** The magnitudes deserve more caution than the mechanisms.

The main effect is large and I find it credible in direction, since a threefold gap between timely and eventual abstention, and sub-0.5 overall recall for most systems, is too big to be an artefact of a metric quirk. The direction of the scaffold effect is also credible, at roughly 0.38 versus 0.18.

The CONVOLVE headline of 26.7 to 57.4 is a big number resting on a small foundation: 20 training trajectories, one epoch, a 101-example held-out set, one base-model family, and no repeated runs. On 101 examples, a shift of 57.4 to 55.3 between the two playbook sources is well inside what noise could produce, so the "small models teach large models" reading is directionally suggestive rather than established. The plain in-context-learning baseline reaching 55.1 against CONVOLVE's 57.4 on the 70B model further suggests that much of the WebShop gain comes from *telling the agent about abstention at all* rather than from trajectory-derived curation specifically. The Appendix G terminal numbers separate CONVOLVE from that baseline more convincingly, at 68.9 against 43.2, and I would weight those higher than the headline.

The 21 environment-based terminal tasks cannot support a percentage to one decimal place, and the paper prints them anyway. The absence of any variance estimate anywhere is the single largest measurement weakness.

Finally, the reasoning and scale results are the ones I would hold most loosely. Each rests on a single model family in a single scenario, and the reasoning claim rests on one Instruct-versus-Thinking pair plus one three-point effort sweep. The *shape* the authors report, that reasoning helps timely stopping and can hurt eventual stopping, is a claim about a trade-off from very few points.

## How it could serve a harness-design effort

Several things here are directly usable by anyone building a loop that must decide whether its work is done, failing, or beyond it.

**The stopping decision should be an explicit typed action, not an inference from prose.** All three of the paper's environments give the agent a first-class abstain action, which is a tool call in WebShop, an enum value in QA, and a flag on the normal response object in Terminal-Bench. The measurement benefit is obvious, but the design benefit is larger: a harness can only route, log, escalate or count a stop that the agent declares in a machine-readable way. The Terminal-Bench form is the most transferable, since it attaches `abstain`, `abstain_category` and `abstain_reason` to the response the agent already emits, so no separate channel is needed.

**Measure lateness, not just correctness.** A harness that records only whether a run ended in a stop is blind to the failure this paper identifies as dominant. The (timely, eventual) pair plus an SPL-style path-length discount is cheap to compute from any trajectory log, given a definition of the earliest step at which the evidence for stopping was present.

**Budget exhaustion is not a stopping decision, and the paper is careful about this.** Episodes terminate either by the agent's choice or by the ten-step cap, and the cap counts as a failure to abstain. A harness that relies on a turn limit as its stopping mechanism has not solved the problem; it has only bounded the cost of not solving it.

**Longer loops cut both ways.** Finding 6 is the most operationally important result for harness design. Extending the budget raises eventual abstention on impossible tasks *and* raises spurious abstention on possible ones, reaching 34% over-abstention by turn 10 in the web setting. Any harness change that gives the loop more room needs to be evaluated against solvable controls, not only against the failure cases it was meant to fix.

**A short natural-language stopping playbook in the system prompt is a high-leverage, low-cost intervention.** CONVOLVE needs no fine-tuning, 20 trajectories, and a rule list short enough to read in a page. A harness can maintain such a list as a first-class artefact, and the transfer result suggests it need not be generated by the strongest available model.

**Repeated actions and invalid actions are usable trajectory signals.** The learned playbook independently converged on search-and-click loops and on ignored invalid-action responses as markers of a missed stopping point, which supports treating those trajectory signatures as harness-level triggers rather than leaving them to the model's own judgement.

**The scaffold is a lever, and a larger one than model choice within this evidence.** Swapping Codex CLI for Terminus 2 roughly doubled abstention recall at fixed model. That argues for treating stopping behaviour as a property of the harness rather than a property to be procured with a better model.

**The fabrication failure is the one to instrument for.** The terminal transcript in Appendix H shows an agent that correctly diagnosed missing grounding in its own reasoning text and then invented a value rather than stopping. A harness that can detect the moment where a required input was not found is positioned to intervene exactly there, and that moment is visible in the trajectory before the fabricated value propagates.

### Limitations and major concerns

- **No variance estimates anywhere.** No error bars, no seeds, no significance tests, and apparently single runs. Combined with an N of 21 for environment-based terminal tasks and 101 for held-out CONVOLVE evaluation, several reported differences cannot be distinguished from noise.
- **The oracle warranted step is assumed, not measured.** Setting `w_i = 2` for all environment-based tasks presumes that one interaction always suffices to reveal a missing target or prerequisite. On a real terminal task, establishing that a dependency is genuinely unavailable may legitimately take several commands, and any such case is scored as a late abstention when it may have been a timely one. No annotation study or agreement statistic supports this constant.
- **Prompts announce abstention, so this measures compliance more than autonomous recognition.** Every system prompt tells the agent that abstaining is available and describes when to use it, and the QA prompt goes further by instructing the model to abstain rather than search forever. This is a reasonable way to make the action observable, but it means the paper measures a prompted capability. It does not tell us how often an agent would recognise futility unprompted, which is closer to the situation in an ordinary harness.
- **Folding clarification into abstention loses a distinction a harness needs.** Stopping because a task is impossible and pausing to ask the user a question are operationally different events with different costs and different next steps. The paper counts both as `ABSTAIN` and terminates the episode on either. A harness cares a great deal which one occurred, and the paper's numbers cannot separate them.
- **Rewriting artefacts.** The request-based tasks are LLM rewrites of solvable tasks, produced by GPT-5.4-mini, and GPT-5.4-mini is also among the evaluated systems and is the fixed base model for the whole terminal setting. The authors mitigate this with manual review and with distributional checks on length and embedding similarity, and the strict validation rules in Appendix I are genuinely well designed. The generator-evaluator overlap remains unaddressed.
- **Coverage of infeasibility is narrow in exactly the interesting direction.** Environment-based abstention only ever means an *absent* resource. The authors themselves name conflicting tool outputs and permission boundaries as untested, and conflicting evidence is the harder and more common real case.
- **CONVOLVE's over-abstention cost is not reported.** The method is trained on abstention-only subsets and scored on abstention metrics. Given finding 6, that pushing an agent toward stopping raises false stops, the omission of an over-abstention or task-success number for the playbook-augmented agents is the paper's most consequential reporting gap.
- **Only two model families carry CONVOLVE**, both Llama-3.3, so nothing establishes that a playbook transfers across families rather than within one.
- **Not peer-reviewed**, and only v1 exists.

## Five citations worth chasing

1. **Kirichenko, Ibrahim, Chaudhuri, and Bell (2025), "AbstentionBench: Reasoning LLMs Fail on Unanswerable Questions"** (arXiv:2506.09038). The direct single-turn predecessor, the source of 16 of this paper's datasets and of its QA category taxonomy. Needed to establish whether the "reasoning does not help" result is inherited from the single-turn setting or arises from the sequential one.
2. **Bonagiri, Kumaragurum, Nguyen, and Plaut (2025), "Check Yourself Before You Wreck Yourself: Selectively Quitting Improves LLM Agent Safety"** (arXiv:2510.16492). The nearest prior work on agents quitting, framed as safety rather than abstention, and reported to show that explicit quit instructions improve safety at limited helpfulness cost. That is the same intervention shape as CONVOLVE, measured against a different objective.
3. **Xie, Gopinath, Qiu, Lin, Sun, Potdar, and Dhingra (2026), "Over-searching in Search-Augmented Large Language Models"** (arXiv:2601.05503). Reported to find that search improves accuracy on answerable queries while harming abstention on unanswerable ones, which is the mechanism behind this paper's over-abstention and budget-length findings.
4. **Qian, Acikgoz, Wang, Chen, Sil, Hakkani-Tur, Tur, and Ji (2025), "SMART: Self-Aware Agent for Tool Overuse Mitigation"** (ACL 2025 Findings). Studies wasted tool calls on *solvable* tasks, which is the complement of this paper's late-abstention cost on unsolvable ones. Together they bound the cost of a loop that does not know when to stop.
5. **Zhang, Hu, Upasani, Ma, Hong, et al. (2025), "Agentic Context Engineering: Evolving Contexts for Self-Improving Language Models"** (arXiv:2510.04618). The method CONVOLVE is explicitly derived from, and the source of the reflect-and-curate playbook architecture. Required reading to judge how much of CONVOLVE is new.

*Honourable mention:* **Wen, Yao, Feng, Xu, Tsvetkov, Howe, and Wang (2025), "Know Your Limits: A Survey of Abstention in Large Language Models"** (TACL 13:529-556), by two of this paper's own authors, which supplies the query/model/human-values framing this paper extends into agent settings.
